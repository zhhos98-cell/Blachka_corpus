Liverpool Museum: one order, several documentary lives

An 1887 workshop sequence follows a Liverpool order from manufacture to accounting and dispatch. More than a century later, surviving models reappear in exhibition and collection records.

11 Oct 1887

The Blaschka workshop told its British intermediary that work on “the models for Liverpool” would begin the following week.

23 Nov 1887

A workshop account separated 247 Marks for the Liverpool models from an additional packing estimate.

Dec 1887

One freight case, marked I.B. 268, was routed through M. O. W. Möller in Hamburg for onward delivery to Liverpool. The dispatch letter records an invoice total read in the project OCR as 268 Marks.

2017

A modern account described a trove of Blaschka sea anemones ↗ rediscovered in a drawer at World Museum Liverpool and shown in Manchester Museum’s Object Lessons ↗.

2019

A published collection image identified a World Museum Liverpool specimen as Blaschka catalogue no. 93 ↗.

What the chain closes

Manufacture, workshop accounting, a marked shipment, institutional destination, and modern physical survival can be connected at collection level.

What remains open

The original model count, Liverpool accession numbers, the receipt register, and the object-level bridge between case I.B. 268 and individual surviving models.

1887

Workshop sequence. Corning / Rakow notebook evidence underlies the manufacture, 247-Mark accounting, and I.B. 268 dispatch statements. Page-level public linking remains pending.

1880

H. H. Higgins, Synopsis of an arrangement of invertebrate animals in the Free Public Museum of Liverpool ↗ provides Liverpool institutional and invertebrate-curation context.

Auckland Museum: seven samples and the order that never grew

Thomas Cheeseman approached the Blaschka models cautiously: catalogue first, exchange if possible, then samples. Seven models reached Auckland in 1885. Plans for a much larger order were repeatedly postponed.

9 Sep 1882

Cheeseman asked Ward for a priced Blaschka catalogue and said he would prefer to obtain models by exchange if possible.

28 Apr 1884

Before committing to a larger purchase, Cheeseman asked for one or two sample models so that he could inspect them first.

3 Apr 1885

Ward described an Adams-linked £20 settlement covering glass models and minerals. Payment moved through a triangular debt arrangement rather than a simple workshop invoice.

1885

Charles Adams safely delivered the Blaschka models. The surviving group reconstructed in the 2019 study contains seven models with Blaschka catalogue numbers, individual Ward prices and Auckland registration numbers.

1885–87

Cheeseman considered a much larger order, first up to £50 and later £40–£50, but repeatedly diverted funds elsewhere. By February 1887 he was reserving money for enlargement of the museum building.

2019

Gill, Grenfell and Blom published the seven-object crosswalk and reconstructed the correspondence linking Cheeseman, Ward and the 1885 supply.

Catalogue enquiry, sample strategy, a seven-model 1885 supply, Ward catalogue numbers, individual prices, Auckland registration numbers, and the Adams-linked payment network.

The exact delivery date, a dedicated seven-model invoice, whether all seven travelled together, the identity relationship between ML35749 and MA124293, and the present display or storage status.

Sample 003

Florence · late 19th c.

A systematic teaching collection whose historical catalogue, surviving count, item-level price and conservation history can be kept separate.

1891 → 2023

Florence: 118 catalogued, 111 surviving, one model priced at 10 lire

Pietro Marchi assembled the Istituto Tecnico’s marine-invertebrate teaching series through multiple purchases from scientific-material dealers. The surviving local catalogue makes it possible to distinguish the original 118-object corpus from the 111 models reported extant in 2017.

Late 19th c.

Marchi selected models from sales catalogues to build a systematic teaching survey of principal European marine invertebrates. The collection was formed through multiple purchases, not a single order.

1891

The last currently certain item-level acquisition is Tubipora hemprichi, MFST Cat. I no. 85 / Inventario I no. 245, bought from a Florentine dealer for 10 lire.

2017

A local museum study reconstructed the complete historical sequence MFST Catalogo I nos. 1–118, reported 111 surviving models, and recorded seven original objects as missing.

Conservation

The collection underwent restoration with technical contribution from the Opificio delle Pietre Dure. Published work includes radiography and FTIR, cleaning, consolidation, colour fixing and re-adhesion; missing portions were deliberately left unreconstructed.

Feb 2017

About fifteen glass objects were documented in 3D during a 13–17 February campaign, with the longer-term aim of extending documentation across the collection.

2023

A Florence municipal cultural notice described newly reinstalled first-floor rooms that again included the Blaschka marine animals. The exact number then on display was not published.

What the chain closes

Collection formation at programme level, a complete 1–118 local historical sequence, the 118/111 count distinction, one exact 1891 price-and-inventory crosswalk, whole-collection conservation and later 3D/public-display activity.

What remains open

The exact first purchase date, dealer and payment chain for most objects, the identities of the seven missing numbers, and the exact current display count.

Tufts: a collection lost on paper before it was found again

The Tufts case turns on custody and institutional memory. P. T. Barnum purchased Blaschka sea creatures for the Barnum Museum in 1885. Decades later, part of the collection was off site at Corning when fire destroyed Barnum Hall, helping produce a long-lived belief that the models themselves had vanished.

1885

Tufts Archival Research Center records that P. T. Barnum purchased Blaschka sea creatures for exhibition in the Barnum Museum of Natural History. The original number, vendor and price remain unresolved.

1962–65

Correspondence with the Corning Museum of Glass began by 1962; a 1965 Russell Carpenter letter asked Corning to take Tufts’ Blaschka models into long-term loan custody.

1972–75

Corning’s 1972 flood damaged documentation and affected stored material. In 1975 Barnum Hall burned. The off-site loan and damaged institutional memory helped turn the collection into a false-loss history.

2006–07

Andrew McClellan rediscovered the archival correspondence in 2006. Tufts asked for 10 models back in 2007; Corning found nine in storage and the tenth, a Portuguese Man O’ War, already on display. All ten were conserved and returned.

2008

Additional Blaschka invertebrates surfaced in a Dana Biology Lab supply closet and were transferred to the archives. Stephen Koob also worked on these rediscovered pieces.

2022–25

Tufts published an exact total of 24 in 2022; TARC published 22 in July 2025 and described them in climate-controlled storage. The two counts are preserved as a live discrepancy rather than reconciled by assumption.

What the chain closes

An 1885 named purchaser and purpose, long-term Corning custody, flood/fire context, documentary rediscovery, a complete 10-model return event, a second campus rediscovery, conservation and a recent archival endpoint.

What remains open

The original 1885 count, seller or intermediary, invoice and price, exact transfer date to Corning, object-level identifiers, the Dana subset count, and the reason for the 24-to-22 count change.

2025

Tufts Archival Research Center, Kate McNally, “Blaschka Beach Vacation” ↗

2016

2009

2022

The 1975 fire is treated as a history of presumed loss, not as proof that the returned Corning subset survived the fire in situ: those models were already off site.

Sample 005

Michigan · 1890s–2019

Two acquisition strata became one fragmentary teaching collection, then a subject of exhibition history.

1890s → 2019

Michigan: one collection, at least two acquisition histories

University of Michigan sources separate the present Blaschka material into at least two historical strata: models that arrived in the 1890s as teaching apparatus, and another group gifted by Harvard University to the Museum of Zoology in 1928.

1890s

Some Blaschka glass creatures came to Michigan as teaching models used within the scientific apparatus of biology. Their purchaser, vendor, price and exact number remain unidentified.

1928

A second group was gifted by Harvard University to the Museum of Zoology on the occasion of the opening of the Ruthven Museums Building. The outgoing Harvard and incoming Michigan object registers have not yet been cross-walked.

Late 20th c.

As newer visualization and teaching technologies displaced glass models, much of the collection moved out of routine instructional use and into storage.

Mar–May 2017

The Museum of Vitreous Ecology brought together the remaining Michigan models that were not then on permanent display. The curators described the collection as approximately 78 marine-invertebrate models, many surviving only as fragments.

Oct–Dec 2017

Object Lessons, the final public exhibition in the Ruthven building, published a checklist with 16 named Blaschka entries. Those exhibition position codes are not museum accession numbers.

2019

The present Museum of Natural History opened in the Biological Sciences Building. The public record recovered so far does not establish how the old Ruthven Blaschka display and stored material map onto the post-2019 building.

What the chain closes

Two acquisition strata at collection level, a named 1928 Harvard-to-Michigan transfer, a 2017 collection-scale estimate and fragmentation statement, and two documented 2017 exhibition uses including a 16-entry checklist.

What remains open

The 1890s transaction, quantities in each stratum, Harvard outgoing and Michigan incoming identifiers, exact intact-versus-fragment counts, and the present object-by-object locations after the 2019 museum move.

2017

Kerstin Barndt and Alice Goff, “The Museum of Vitreous Ecology: Blaschka Glass Models at Michigan” ↗

2017

University of Michigan, Object Lessons — Teaching Models object checklist ↗

The broader 2017 curatorial suggestion that Michigan material may derive from Harvard’s Agassiz/MCZ collection remains a provenance hypothesis. The stronger public statement is narrower: some models arrived locally in the 1890s; others were gifted by Harvard in 1928.

Mexico City: one nineteenth-century collection, two successor endpoints

Mexico’s Blaschka history is strongest as a story of institutional fission. An 1885 workshop mailing names the national museum, an 1895 visitor independently confirms a substantial physical Blaschka display, and later natural-history reorganisation leaves identifiable glass animals at both UNAM and the city’s Museo de Historia Natural y Cultura Ambiental.

Jul 1885

A Blaschka workshop catalogue-mailing sequence contains an OCR reading normalised here as “Museo Nacional Mexico”. This proves contact or solicitation, not a completed order.

1895

Frank Collins Baker described a “very good collection of glass models, by Blaschka” in the Museo Nacional, including sea cucumbers, sea anemones and other invertebrates displayed in one case.

1913–29

The national natural-history collections moved into the El Chopo museum system. In 1915 they entered the Dirección de Estudios Biológicos; in 1929 historical collections passed into the newly founded Instituto de Biología at UNAM.

1964

The new Chapultepec natural-history museum inherited part of the old Chopo material while other historical collections remained within the UNAM biological-collection system. The Blaschka corpus therefore has to be reconstructed across successor institutions.

2023–26

UNAM publicly identifies an Animales de Cristal Blaschka collection. The city museum separately names the historical glass Argonauta argo and Pelagia noctiluca among Chopo-derived exhibition objects.

What the chain closes

Workshop contact by 1885, a substantial physical national-museum display by 1895, the institutional succession through El Chopo and UNAM, and present Blaschka-labelled material at two successor endpoints.

What remains open

The exact acquisition date, order route, price and original count; the object-by-object split between UNAM and the 1964 city museum; local identifiers; and a published dating conflict for the two city-museum glass animals.

A securely surviving collection with repeated exhibition and technical-study checkpoints, but an acquisition history that remains largely blank.

Newcastle: a visible collection with an invisible purchase history

The Great North Museum: Hancock can repeatedly be seen using its Blaschka marine models in exhibitions and technical imaging. The nineteenth-century transaction that brought them to Newcastle, however, has not yet surfaced in the public record.

1884

The Natural History Society of Northumbria opened its new natural-history museum in Newcastle. The institution later became the Hancock Museum and, after redevelopment, the Great North Museum: Hancock.

2008–09

An NHSN report records the Society’s glass sea-anemone collection by Leopold and Rudolf Blaschka on display at the National Glass Centre in Sunderland.

2009

The redeveloped Great North Museum opened, bringing the historic Hancock collections into a new institutional and display framework.

2013

A Newcastle University research exhibition used an X-ray image of a Blaschka glass jellyfish, turning a surviving object into evidence for internal construction as well as external form.

2025

Imitating Life presented the museum’s collection of Blaschka glass marine-invertebrate models as a dedicated exhibition until 21 September 2025.

What the chain closes

Institutional continuity from the nineteenth-century museum to the Great North Museum, surviving sea-anemone and jellyfish material, technical imaging in 2013, and a collection-scale exhibition in 2025.

What remains open

The purchaser or donor, acquisition date, dealer or intermediary, original count, local object identifiers, and exact current survival/display totals. A secondary report of about fifty refers only to sea anemones and is not treated as the collection total.

Nottingham: 356 in the ledger, but not 356 models

Two workshop-side records make Nottingham unusually instructive. One records an 1887 “Museum Nottingham” model account with the figure 356. Another shows John Wesley Carr being told that the workshop had promised Damon it would not accept direct English commissions. The surviving museum collection therefore sits inside a dealer-agency structure that the raw ledger alone cannot explain.

1886–87

In correspondence associated with John Wesley Carr of the Nottingham Natural History Museum / University College, the workshop explained that it had promised Damon not to undertake direct commissions for England.

1887

The workshop account contains an OCR reading normalised as “Museum Nottingham — Modelle — 356”. The surrounding annual account is denominated in Marks, so 356 is treated as an account-value candidate, not a model count.

1926

Nottingham’s municipal natural-history museum moved into Wollaton Hall. A Blaschka-specific 1926 transfer list has not yet been recovered.

Object anchor

Nottingham Museums publishes a surviving Blaschka model of an octopus, INV 2000, with a separate 1890 date layer. That object is evidence of local survival, not yet an object-level bridge to the 1887 account.

2025–26

The Endangered Planet gallery material identifies a selection of Blaschka glass models of sea invertebrates. Wollaton Hall marks its centenary as Nottingham Natural History Museum in 2026.

What the chain closes

An 1887 museum account, Carr’s place inside the English Damon agency problem, municipal continuity to Wollaton Hall, at least one published local identifier, and plural recent display evidence.

What remains open

What the ledger’s 356 precisely denotes, the original order/list and payment route, the 1926 transfer crosswalk, exact survival count, and the object-level relation between INV 2000 and the 1887 transaction.

1887

Workshop records. Rakow notebook OCR preserves the “Museum Nottingham” account and Carr/Damon agency correspondence. Image-level verification remains a priority before quoting the original handwriting publicly.

Vassar: an 1887 teaching purchase rescued from institutional drift

Vassar’s public record gives a rare firm acquisition year without yet giving the invoice. The models were purchased in 1887, survived into the Biology Department, entered a Corning-linked conservation programme, and later reappeared during campus artefact rescue and exhibition work.

1887

Vassar institutional sources state that Blaschka invertebrate models were purchased for the college in 1887 as part of its natural-history collection. The seller, price and exact original count remain open.

2010

A Vassar museum newsletter located the models in the Biology Department and described an arrangement with Stephen Koob of the Corning Museum of Glass to repair and restore them.

2013

The Vassar College Artifacts Project recovered glass replicas of jellyfish and other organisms by the Blaschkas during renovation of Swift Hall.

2016

Art at Vassar illustrated Jelly Fish no. 229, glass on a metal mount, and explicitly labelled it “Purchased for Vassar College, 1887.” The number is treated as a likely commercial/model number, not a verified Vassar accession.

2018

Vassar again described a plural collection of invertebrates created by the Blaschkas. An exact current collection total was not supplied.

What the chain closes

A firm 1887 purchase year, twenty-first-century physical survival, Corning conservation involvement, a documented campus rescue, and a named illustrated object tied back to the 1887 purchase.

What remains open

The 1887 seller/intermediary, invoice, price and original count; local accession numbers; the exact conservation register; present storage/display totals; and the extent to which no. 229 can be cross-walked to Vassar’s internal records.

Milwaukee: seventy models, one Ward route, one unresolved overlap

Milwaukee Public Museum supplies an unusually firm historical quantity: the museum says it purchased seventy Blaschka invertebrate glass models offered through Ward’s Natural Science Establishment. What remains uncertain is whether those seventy were part of a much larger Ward natural-history acquisition funded in 1883–84.

1883–84

A large Ward natural-history collection was acquired for Milwaukee through public subscription for a reported $12,000 and displayed as the new museum opened in 1884. The project does not yet treat the seventy Blaschka models as proven components of that purchase.

Historical purchase

Milwaukee Public Museum explicitly states that it purchased 70 invertebrate glass models offered for sale through Ward’s Natural Science Establishment.

1987

Joan P. Jass published an MPM-specific study comparing the museum’s Blaschka models with Ward’s 1888 catalogue. The full concordance has not yet been recovered into this project.

2023–24

MPM’s annual reporting provides a recent object/media anchor, Glaucilla briareus, described as a Blaschka glass sea slug. A candidate relation to Blaschka catalogue no. 446 remains an external crosswalk, not a local identification.

2027

The collection is moving toward the future Nature & Culture Museum of Wisconsin. A future Blaschka display has not yet been publicly confirmed, so the 2027 endpoint remains a custody transition rather than a display claim.

What the chain closes

A firm seventy-model historical purchase count, Ward as supplier/intermediary, continued institutional custody, an MPM-specific catalogue study, and a recent named object anchor.

What remains open

The exact purchase/receipt date, invoice and price for the seventy models, whether they belong inside the $12,000 Ward acquisition, the Jass object list, local identifiers, exact current survival count, and post-move display status.
