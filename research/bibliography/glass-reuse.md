# Glass reuse, recycling, and object continuity — working bibliography

This supplementary research README preserves the bibliography and conceptual prompts supplied with the CFP for the **4ème colloque international, _Les multiples vies du verre: Histoire de recyclages depuis l'Antiquité_**, Orléans, 3–5 June 2027.

It is intentionally separate from the repository's existing `README.md` files. The original root README and `research/README.md` are unchanged.

**Source note.** Bibliographic details below are transcribed from the CFP text supplied on 9 August 2026. Line breaks and punctuation have been lightly normalized for readability; apparent errors, unusual spellings, dates, and page ranges in the CFP have not been silently corrected or reconciled against external catalogues.

## Why this bibliography matters to the project

The CFP uses "recycling" in several distinct senses that are useful beyond the immediate conference proposal:

- **material recycling**: cullet or broken glass returning to production;
- **lateral reuse / reusing**: a shaped glass object changes hands while form and sometimes function remain stable;
- **détournement / repurposing**: a shaped glass object acquires an unanticipated use, with or without physical transformation;
- **object ontology and continuity**: which attributes can change while an artefact is still treated as the same object;
- **circulation and recovery systems**: collection, storage, transport, transaction costs, regulation, ownership, and institutional handling;
- **material traces of reuse**: wear, breakage, repairs, mounts, labels, coverings, fillers, and other additions.

For the microscopy corpus, these categories help connect remounting, recutting, exchange, postal circulation, packing, reusable fillers, boxes, labels, specimen transfer, and return loops without reducing the problem to damage or failure. A microscope slide is a composite object whose glass support, specimen, medium, cover, label, container, and packing materials can follow different material lives.

For the Blaschka project, the same literature provides a comparative vocabulary for long-lived shaped glass objects, conservation, changing functions, institutional transfers, and the shift from replaceable teaching or display models to historically irreplaceable objects.

### Working relevance

The most immediately useful conceptual pair for the microscopy work is **Schiffer + Smail/Pizzorno/Hay**: formation processes, reuse, and the problem of what remains the “same object” when material, form, condition, supplement, content, value, owner, or function changes. Demoule is useful for the historical production of replaceability/irreplaceability.

## CFP prompts worth preserving

The conference text also proposes several practical evidential questions that can be reused in our own research design:

- compare composition with recipes and contemporary technical treatises;
- identify spatial and temporal markers and moments of transition;
- reconstruct collection and circulation by maritime, fluvial, and terrestrial routes;
- track regulation, taxation, tolls, transaction costs, market value, and economic management;
- identify actors: large owners, religious or lay institutions, craftspeople, firms, wholesalers, retailers;
- examine storage archaeologically: cargoes, urban or rural spaces, public/religious complexes, workshops, domestic spaces;
- record packing/storage form: loose, amphora, barrel, basket, box, wrapper, filler, rack, and any sorting by size, colour, quality, or type;
- use marks, labels, traces of wear, breakage, mounts, covers, and added devices as evidence for previous use;
- distinguish preservation, reuse, repair, transfer, repurposing, and transformation rather than treating them as one undifferentiated “afterlife”.

## Priority reading queue for the current microscopy angle

1. **Daniel Lord Smail, Gabriel H. Pizzorno, Nathaniel Hay** — object ontology under reuse; which attributes can change while continuity is maintained.
2. **M. B. Schiffer** — formation processes and the material consequences of movement, discard, recovery, reuse, and deposition.
3. **Jane Busch** — second-hand bottle reuse and the separation of form, original function, and final context.
4. **Tom Licence** and **Nicolas Marty** — return systems, regulation, standardisation, and the organised infrastructure required for reuse.
5. **Jean-Paul Demoule** — replaceability and the production of irreplaceable objects.
6. **Tania Chinni et al.** — synthetic vocabulary for cycles, recycles, and reuses of glass.

## Current project question

A productive working question emerging from the microscopy corpus is:

> When a microscope preparation travelled, was remounted, returned, repaired, relabelled, or repacked, what exactly was being kept continuous, and which components were allowed to be replaced or reused?

The aim is to reconstruct **material continuity as an organised practice**, including transport and packing, rather than to reduce the history to breakage, failure, or a generic “afterlife” of scientific objects.
