# Rudolf 1892 specimen–model divergence, dispatch and value pass 14 — 12 August 2026

## Purpose

Continue the transport investigation after passes 11–13 by pushing from shipment-level reconstruction to specimen/model crosswalk, dispatch infrastructure and circulation accounting. The public map is left unchanged in this pass. The aim is to distinguish what is now source-closed from what remains a retrieval target.

This pass produces four main results:

1. The pass-13 `50/51` problem for the January 1894 ninth shipment is resolved: the shipment is **model nos. 424–473 inclusive, exactly 50 models**. Model 474 begins the tenth shipment.
2. Model **433, Hoffmannseggia**, is now a high-confidence specimen→model crosswalk linking an 1892 Tempe herbarium specimen retained at Harvard to a January 1894 glass model made at Hosterwitz and returned to Harvard. It is the strongest current microhistory of **representational divergence and later reaggregation**.
3. The 1893–94 papers show a separate **dispatch clock**: completed models could still wait on mounting, crate manufacture, packing, insurance and consular certification before they could move.
4. The 1894 workshop notebook separates model valuation from cases, cartons, packing labour, local transport, insurance and consular certification. Circulation therefore acquired an explicit accounting structure, and the contract separately assigned transport/insurance/consular expenses to Harvard.

The revised mechanism is:

> **Transport coupled a split workshop by converting plants and models into handleable, insurable, certifiable and countable units. Those conversions shaped both what survived as evidence and what later appeared as a coherent shipment.**

A field plant could produce multiple durable descendants held in different places: herbarium sheet, drawing/study, living or preserved material, and later glass model. Reassembly was selective rather than total.

## 1. Ninth shipment boundary corrected: 424–473 = 50

Pass 13 deliberately left a discrepancy open because one OCR of the annotated shipment list appeared to place the `10th Shipment, August, 1894` heading after model 474, producing a 51-number span for a letter that said 50 species.

A cleaner OCR of the same Harvard annotated shipment list resolves the boundary. Near the end of the ninth shipment it reads the final entries through the Phthirusa/Loranthus line, then:

`10th Shipment, August, 1894.`

followed by:

`474. Sanguinaria canadensis, Linn`
`475. Caltha palustris, Linn`

Thus the January 1894 ninth shipment is **424–473 inclusive = exactly 50 numbered models**. The apparent extra `Loranthus pauciflorus` in the poorer OCR is a synonym/continuation attached to model 473, not model 474.

This aligns with two independent direct sources:

- BLA-D00222 / 015:224, 15 Dec 1893: January consignment `composed of 50 species`.
- BLA-D00237 / 015:240, Goodale: astonished at the `new invoice of 50 plants`.

**Correction to pass 13:** close the `50/51 unresolved` issue. Do not use 424–474 for the ninth shipment.

The cleaner boundary simultaneously establishes model **474 Sanguinaria canadensis** as the first numbered model of the tenth shipment.

## 2. Model 433: first strong specimen→model divergence chain

### 2.1 Field specimen retained at Harvard

The official Harvard University Herbaria specimen database records:

- GH barcode **01676890**
- accepted name **Hoffmannseggia glauca (Ortega) Eifert**
- USA, Arizona, **Tempe?**
- collectors **W. F. Ganong & R. Blaschka**
- date **1892-4**

The specimen therefore belongs directly to the documented Tempe phase of the 1892 journey and survives at Harvard rather than being inferred from correspondence.

Official Kew POWO currently treats **Hoffmannseggia stricta Benth.** and **Hoffmannseggia densiflora Benth.** as synonyms of accepted **Hoffmannseggia glauca**. This resolves the historical-name drift needed for comparison with the Glass Flowers documentation.

### 2.2 Glass model 433

The Harvard Glass Flowers label/finding-aid layer identifies **model 433** historically as `Hoffmanseggia densiflora (Hoffmanseggia stricta)` and in the updated nomenclature as `Hoffmannseggia glauca [syn H. densiflora]`. The project should still prefer the original digitized Harvard label image for publication-level citation, but the current archival/export layer is consistent with the HUH specimen and modern Kew synonymy.

The January 1894 workshop notebook's model sequence has model 433 at the corresponding tenth item of the ninth shipment; OCR renders the name approximately `Hoffmannsgia striata`. Given the Harvard model label and taxonomic synonymy, `striata` is treated as an OCR error, not silently as a historical taxon.

Rudolf's 7 Aug 1900 authorship letter provides a further direct object-level witness. In his list of mostly smaller models that he says were `constructed by myself alone`, he includes **`433. Hoffmannergia`**. He immediately qualifies this class by saying that the analytical details were mostly his father's work. The guarded maker statement is therefore: **Rudolf retrospectively claimed sole construction of model 433's main model, while analytical details were generally Leopold's share.**

### 2.3 Why this is analytically important

This is close to a closed split-reference biography:

`living Arizona plant → Ganong/Rudolf field collection → herbarium specimen retained at Harvard`

and, across the Atlantic:

`field study/reference relation → Hosterwitz manufacture → model 433 → January 1894 shipment → Harvard`

The plant does not simply travel once and become a model. One descendant remains as a dried specimen in the herbarium while another descendant is manufactured later in glass and sent back to the same institutional complex. The key object is therefore not an original that moves intact but a **reference lineage that diverges and later coexists**.

Guard: the specimen→model relation is high-confidence by collector/date/place + exact model number/name/synonym + shipment chronology, but a publication should still inspect the original model-433 label and, if possible, the original specimen label/image before phrasing it as a fully closed one-to-one modelling source.

## 4. The dispatch clock: finishing is not dispatch

BLA-D00168 / 015:169, 18 Jul 1893, says the models of the current consignment are `now all ready` except some mounting work. Yet the Blaschkas say the shipping day is hard to know because the **packing boxes must be ordered at the last moment**, and the date depends on `the man who makes them`; this can shift packing by one or two weeks.

This isolates a **dispatch clock** distinct from the production clock. A model could be finished as a representation but not yet be dispatchable. Departure depended on mounting and on an external crate-maker.

BLA-D00169 / 015:170 adds the support mechanism. `Most of these models` had again been fixed on **glass panes because it should be impossible to handle those fragile things in another way**. The support was therefore not simply protection against breakage. It converted a fragile model into a **handleable object state** before crating and shipment.

This dovetails with the later 1930 transport method, in which temporary support, inner box, immobilisation, outer case, customs opening and museum remounting form a full transformation sequence. The 1893 letter proves that this logic was already explicit at the level of handling.

## 5. Tenth shipment: a second fifty-model consignment with exact case marks

The corrected annotated-list boundary puts model 474 at the start of the tenth shipment. The August 1894 workshop account enumerates **50 items**, ending with names corresponding to models:

- 519 *Antirrhinum coulterianum*
- 520 *Gilia californica*
- 521 *Houstonia caerulea*
- 522 *Allium serratum*
- 523 *Calopogon pulchellus*

This strongly establishes the tenth shipment as **474–523 inclusive = 50 models**.

BLA-D00247 / 015:250, 28 Aug 1894, gives the physical consignment:

- **four cases**
- case marks **I.B. 366, 367, 368, 369**
- forwarded to Bremen
- specific unpacking warning for fragile palm leaves and small Compositae.

Thus 1894 now contains two consecutive 50-model shipment structures:

- 9th shipment, January 1894: 424–473 = 50.
- 10th shipment, August 1894: 474–523 = 50; four cases I.B.366–369.

The identical count should not be mistaken for identical production conditions. BLA-D00247 says the summer shipment had involved exceptional trouble and delay, while Goodale's 2 Sep letter says a July list had slipped into September and worries that the Blaschkas were working beyond their strength.

## 6. Shipment size itself was negotiable

BLA-D00250 / 015:253, 2 Sep 1894, shows Goodale responding to the delayed summer shipment by proposing that the Wares authorize him to tell the Blaschkas that **30 or 40 models** would be satisfactory for the next January instalment. He interprets the delay as evidence that the Blaschkas have reached the limit of their power.

BLA-D00260 / 015:263, 3 Nov 1894, records the Blaschkas' gratitude for permission to **reduce the number of models in the next shipment**. They explain that some American specimens now in hand require far more time than anticipated; an individual specimen can take weeks, forcing the shipment date to wait until the specified count is reached.

This gives a strong causal chain:

`material difficulty → model-specific production time → inability to hit fixed shipment count → dispatch delay → receiver/patron renegotiates expected count`.

The shipment is therefore not only an aggregation technology. It is also a negotiated scheduling device linking object difficulty to labour capacity and institutional expectation.

## 7. Contract and circulation accounting: salary, model valuation and logistics are different layers

BLA-D01210 / 018:80 preserves a copy/OCR of the 1890 exclusive contract. Its core terms are:

- **8,800 marks per year**
- paid in **two half-year instalments of 4,400 marks**
- **two shipments each year**
- Harvard Botanical Department to defray **all expenses from place of manufacture to Cambridge**, including **insurance** and **consular certificate**.

BLA-D00231 / 015:234 independently preserves a **4,400-mark check dated 10 Jan 1894**, consistent with the half-year payment structure.

The workshop notebook/account pages around the 1894 consignments then separate the circulation side into explicit categories. The August 1894 account is arithmetically complete:

- `Modelle` — **4240 M**
- `4 Kisten` — **50 M**
- `Cartons` — **55 M**
- `Emballieren` — **30.30 M**
- `Transport nach Dresden` — **11.50 M**
- `Versicherung bis Bremen` — **2.50 M**
- `Consular Beglaubigung` — **10.70 M**
- total — **4400 M**

The non-model circulation lines sum to **160 M**, while the model line is 4240 M.

The January 1894 account likewise reads `Modelle 4240` and total `4400`, with visible lines for four cases, packing, transport to Dresden, insurance to Bremen and consular certification. OCR of at least one additional charge is unreliable, so the missing difference is left unidentified rather than silently reconstructed.

### Accounting guard

The coincidence between the notebook total `4400` and the contractual half-year instalment `4400` is striking, but the present evidence does **not** establish that the notebook's `Modelle 4240 + circulation charges 160 = 4400` is the same thing as salary/payment in a simple price sense. The contract says Harvard pays the 4400 instalment and separately defrays transport/insurance/consular expenses. Goodale also speaks of `invoice` for the incoming plants. The notebook may therefore be translating model and circulation value into an invoice/consular/accounting format distinct from the labour contract.

Do not call 4240 `production price` or 4400 `sale price` until the source genre and Harvard-side matching invoice are isolated.

The safe finding is stronger anyway: **the archive keeps model value, physical packing, local carriage, insurance and consular certification as separable accounting categories.** A shipment acquired several values depending on which transaction/document was being made.

## 8. Receiving-side accounting remains to be closed

BLA-D00239 / 015:242, 19 Mar 1894, says Goodale had just received **Mr Snow's bill for commission and expenses on the importation** and believed it correct; an official/inspector then came and passed the cases, after which they could be opened at leisure. The exact dollar amount is OCR-unsafe and is not used.

This indicates a second cost/accounting layer at destination: workshop-side cases/packing/transport/insurance/consular certification were followed by broker commission/importation expenses and customs release at Harvard.

The highest-priority archival target remains Harvard's explicitly listed **folder of receipts and bills, 1892**, together with the `Acquisition, Development, and Shipping lists, circa 1890–1982`. The collection also preserves original boxes and mailing material, and Harvard states that most model/section labels are digitized.

## 9. Revised conceptual architecture

The project now has five distinct clocks/layers:

1. **production clock** — making and finishing models;
2. **dispatch clock** — mounting, crate manufacture, packing, documentation and forwarder readiness;
3. **transport/clearance clock** — freight, bond, customs, broker and museum receipt;
4. **reference clock** — specimens, drawings, seeds, living/preserved material and books that can remain elsewhere or arrive later;
5. **shipment/counting clock** — institutional expectation that heterogeneous finished work be aggregated into periodic consignments of a negotiated size.

Model 433 adds a sixth analytical problem: **reference divergence**. One expedition plant can produce durable descendants in different media and repositories. The herbarium sheet and glass model do not form a before/after replacement relation; they coexist as different products of the same field encounter/reference lineage.

The strongest current article-level formulation is:

> **The Blaschka workshop worked across distance by splitting plants and work into differently transportable, documentable and countable forms. Transport did not merely carry finished representations. It helped determine which properties survived, which objects could be handled, how value was declared, when a finished model could leave, and which heterogeneous histories were later compressed into a shipment.**
