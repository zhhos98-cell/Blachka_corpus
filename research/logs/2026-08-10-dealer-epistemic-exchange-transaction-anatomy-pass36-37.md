# Dealer epistemic exchange and transaction anatomy — passes 36–37

## Purpose

This pass continues the evidence-led dealer-source expansion without reopening broad repository discovery. It asks two narrower questions: what dealers did to knowledge as well as to commodities, and what documentary layers a dealer transaction normally generated on the receiving side.

## Pass 36 — dealers as epistemic and exchange infrastructure

Joseph Leidy's March 1880 firsthand account of Ward's Natural Science Establishment is unusually useful because it describes the commercial stock and an actual teaching use in the same text. Ward's buildings held mounted vertebrates, skeletons, invertebrates, models, minerals, rocks and fossils. Leidy bought two red-coral models for one dollar each, one natural size and one magnified, and placed them beside a real coral fragment. The models are not assigned to the Blaschkas because Leidy does not name their maker. The importance is comparative: model and specimen were deliberately combined to make animal form and taxonomic order intelligible.

Nature in 1871 supplies a Damon counterpart. It described Robert Damon as a well-known dealer in natural-history objects and reported that he procured numerous West Indian Pentacrini, enabling detailed observations of hard and soft parts by scientific investigators. The dealer therefore created access to research material, rather than only redistributing knowledge after the fact.

Te Papa preserves a primary archival record in which Damon sought New Zealand tertiary fossils, land shells and animals in spirits in exchange for overseas shells. No date is imposed from the discovery result used here. The record establishes reciprocal exchange as part of his business mechanism. Nature's 1889 obituary and a separate Te Papa letter show Damon also operating at collection scale through the purchase and redistribution of the Godeffroy Museum zoological collections.

Powerhouse object 14532 provides an object-level control: barite purchased from R. Damon on 22 December 1886 still carries an old museum label. Dealer provenance, institutional numbering and physical object can therefore survive as separate layers.

## Pass 37 — transaction anatomy

The next step is methodological. Dealer transactions should not be searched as invoice events. Receiving institutions preserve document bundles whose parts can migrate into different archival series.

The ANSP Conchological Section keeps Damon's 1868 shell invoice inside a broader accounting environment with cash books, ledgers, receipts and vouchers. Missouri Box 7 FF 2G groups Ward correspondence with payment requests and bills of lading. Smithsonian RU000208 has a dedicated Ward's Natural Science Establishment correspondence folder and, separately, an invoice series whose records include specimen description, catalogue number, collector, locality and terms under which material was sent.

Later Ward files at the American Museum of Natural History are retained as longitudinal controls. Mss .H682 Box 4 Folder 206 contains purchase negotiations, postal receipts, an estimate and an account statement; Box 4 Folder 204 contains correspondence, notes, invoices and shipping-insurance certificates. Box 1 Folder 55 separately preserves Ward price lists/catalogues with correspondence. These files postdate the marine-model sales period and are not used as evidence for a nineteenth-century Blaschka shipment. They reveal the documentary repertoire of the same firm and therefore tell us which neighbouring record types to search when a Blaschka transaction is incomplete.

The resulting retrieval vocabulary is: catalogue/price list → enquiry/correspondence → selection list → estimate/quotation → invoice → payment/remittance → receipt → bill of lading/forwarding → insurance → account reconciliation → museum accession/distribution record.

## Analytical consequence

The dealer now appears in the project in four distinct ways. It is an archive problem (where business records survive), a material-supply problem (how stock is assembled), an epistemic problem (how specimens and models are made comparable and available for observation), and a documentary problem (how one transaction is fragmented into different record series).

For Blaschka research this changes the meaning of a provenance gap. 'Invoice missing' is too coarse. A transaction may still be recoverable through a price list, customer selection, remittance, bill of lading, insurance certificate, account reconciliation or accession entry. The next corpus-level task should therefore audit known Blaschka orders against these document types rather than continue general dealer-name searching.
