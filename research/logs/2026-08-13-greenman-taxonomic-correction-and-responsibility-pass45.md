# Greenman taxonomic correction, responsibility and label repair — pass 45 — 13 August 2026

## Purpose

Pass 33 identified `taxonomic latency`: some Jamaica and western references could be materially present in Hosterwitz while their names remained provisional or unresolved. A later source cluster around Jesse M. Greenman now shows that the problem did not end when a model was manufactured. Determinations could later be audited, judged poor, corrected and translated into new labels at additional cost. Taxonomy therefore had a **post-production repair history**.

## Result

Three primary-source layers now fit together:

1. **1893** — `BLA-D00169 / 015:170`: the Blaschkas report that some Jamaica plants remained undetermined after the journey, while some western plants had probably been determined in Cambridge; they cautiously quote names they hope are correct.
2. **1901–1902** — Goodale describes `Mr. Greenman of the Herbarium`, notes that Greenman had visited the Blaschkas, and Rudolf later reports a Goodale letter concerning the `Greenman determining matter`. According to Rudolf, Goodale says that the plants collected on the California journey had been **very carelessly determined**. Rudolf immediately distinguishes those determinations from the microscopic analytical work for which he accepts responsibility.
3. **1930 technical memorandum** — `BLA-D02898 / 040:257`, `Method of preparing the models for Transportation`, states that **most of the critical determinations of the species had been made by Dr. Jesse M. Greenman, of Chicago, formerly of the Gray Herbarium**.

A related Goodale letter of 29 January 1903, `BLA-D01975 / 020:511`, shows that nomenclatural/label errors were being corrected `once for all` and that determination work associated with Greenman consumed substantial time and money; the exact syntax and allocation of the roughly three months / $450 figure are OCR-uncertain and require image verification before actor-specific cost claims.

The strongest formulation is:

> **A glass model could be materially finished while its taxonomic identity remained revisable. Determination was another distributed craft, and mistakes in that layer generated later documentary and financial repair.**

## 1. 1893: cautious naming after return

`BLA-D00169 / 015:170` already prevents a simple field-to-label story. The Blaschkas say they had managed, `though with scanty means`, to find the names of some previously undetermined plants, especially Jamaica material. They then say that some western plants had `probably` meanwhile been determined at Harvard and give names they `hope` are right, including the California *Viola chrysantha*, *Rosa gymnocarpa*, and Missouri *Rosa woodsii*.

The epistemic state is explicit:

`reference available`
→ `identity unresolved/provisional`
→ `distributed determination in Hosterwitz and/or Cambridge`
→ `name used with residual uncertainty`.

This means a model batch and its taxonomy need not mature at the same speed.

---

## 2. Greenman physically crosses the workshop/Herbarium boundary

A Goodale letter dated 1 January 1901, `BLA-D01880 / 020:414`, calls Greenman `of the Herbarium` and says he had made a visit to the Blaschkas and was delighted with Rudolf's work.

This matters because the taxonomic actor was not confined to a distant label desk. Greenman had direct knowledge of the workshop and its objects before the later determination dispute surfaces.

External Harvard/BHL metadata independently identifies the botanist as **Jesse More Greenman (1867–1951)**, an author of numerous *Contributions from the Gray Herbarium* papers in the 1890s–1900, including work on southwestern and Mexican plants. Harvard's Index of Botanists also records him under the standard label name `J. M. Greenman` and gives a Cambridge residence circa 1900.

Guard: the Goodale letter itself prints only `Mr. Greenman of the Herbarium`. Identification with Jesse More Greenman is strongly corroborated by the later 1930 memorandum naming Jesse M. Greenman explicitly, but the physical visit source does not spell out his first names.

---

## 3. February 1902: California determinations are retrospectively challenged

`BLA-D01938–39 / 020:472–473`, Rudolf to Mary Lee Ware, 24 February 1902, gives the strongest direct statement.

Rudolf says that he has just received a letter from Goodale about the `Greenman determining matter`. He reports Goodale's judgment that the plants collected on the California journey had been **very carelessly determined**.

Rudolf then draws a responsibility boundary. He says he is not answerable for this; what he can answer for is the independent analytical work he himself carried out by microscope, in which his companion had no share. He is glad Goodale has acknowledged that distinction.

This is unusually valuable because it separates several epistemic roles that later catalogue labels can make look unified:

- field collection;
- companion/collector participation;
- taxonomic determination;
- Rudolf's microscopic analysis;
- glass manufacture;
- later label/nomenclature stabilization.

The model is therefore a composite product whose different claims have different responsible agents.

---

## 4. Taxonomic error does not erase material success

The 1902 complaint appears roughly a decade after the 1892 expedition and after expedition-derived models had entered Harvard shipments. This shows that a representation could be considered materially successful enough to manufacture, ship and display while its scientific name later required correction.

Taxonomic latency should therefore be expanded into at least two forms:

- **pre-production taxonomic latency** — reference exists but identity is unresolved before/around manufacture;
- **post-production taxonomic revision** — model exists and circulates, but determination or label is later audited and corrected.

The second form matters for object history. A museum object can remain physically the same while its documentary identity changes.

---

## 5. January 1903: correction becomes labour and expenditure

`BLA-D01974–75 / 020:510–511`, Goodale to Ware, 29 January 1903, discusses label/table work and says errors now being corrected `once for all` were connected with his absence in Europe and too much confidence placed in another worker. The passage then refers to Greenman and determination work requiring about three months at `$150` per month, or more than `$450` altogether.

The OCR is too damaged to assign the entire three-month/$450 cost cleanly to Greenman himself or to state exactly who was paid for what. The safe claim is narrower:

> **taxonomy/nomenclature correction was not free metadata maintenance; the archive places determination, label correction, labour time and hundreds of dollars in the same administrative sequence.**

The original image should be read before using the monetary figure in publication.

---

## 6. 1930: Greenman is remembered as the principal critical determiner

The technical memorandum `BLA-D02898 / 040:257`, preserved immediately before Oakes Ames's 15 December 1930 packing letter, describes the entire transformation from Hosterwitz transport mounting to Harvard plaster-plaque remounting. Near the end it adds that **most critical species determinations were made by Dr. Jesse M. Greenman, of Chicago, formerly of the Gray Herbarium**.

This retrospective procedural source is important because it treats determination as part of the collection's operational infrastructure alongside packing, customs opening, remounting and labelling.

The archive therefore remembers Greenman not as an incidental consultant but as a recurrent specialist in turning models into correctly named museum objects.

## 7. New object-state sequence

Add a documentary/taxonomic lane parallel to the physical object lane:

### Physical lane

`reference → glass manufacture → transport mounting → shipment → customs/unpacking → plaster remounting → display`.

### Identity lane

`field/provisional name`
→ `determination attempt`
→ `model label/name`
→ `later audit`
→ `determination corrected/revised`
→ `label/nomenclature repair`.

The two lanes can move asynchronously. A physically stable model can have an unstable name; a correctly labelled model can later undergo physical repair without taxonomic change.

## 8. Responsibility ontology

The 1902 passage suggests explicitly coding **claim responsibility** rather than assigning a whole model to one epistemic author.

Possible fields:

- `field_capture_responsibility`;
- `preservation_responsibility`;
- `taxonomic_determination_responsibility`;
- `microscopic_analysis_responsibility`;
- `glass_manufacture_responsibility`;
- `label/nomenclature_responsibility`;
- `later_revision_responsibility`.

Rudolf himself insists on this partition when defending the quality of his microscope-based analysis against errors in the California plant determinations.

This is a strong HPS point because error attribution follows the division of labour.

---

## 9. Article consequence

Taxonomy should enter the article neither as a preliminary naming step nor as metadata appended after transport. It is an intermittently active layer whose timing can cross fieldwork, workshop production, shipment and later museum administration.

A useful sentence:

> **The model could survive the Atlantic before its name survived scrutiny.**

A more exact formulation:

> **Glass stabilized morphology more readily than the archive stabilized identity: names could lag behind manufacture and could be reopened years after the model entered the collection.**

## 10. Source-critical guards

1. Preserve Rudolf's wording as reported through his 1902 letter: we currently have Rudolf summarizing Goodale's judgment, not Goodale's underlying letter.
2. `California journey` is very likely Rudolf's American expedition material in the Glass Flowers context, but the exact set of specimens/models under review remains unidentified.
3. Do not say Greenman made the `careless` original determinations. The phrase `Greenman determining matter` can equally indicate that Greenman was reviewing/correcting them; the current sources do not assign the initial error securely.
4. Do not assign the 1903 three-month/$450 figure specifically to Greenman's personal fee until the manuscript image resolves the syntax.
5. The 1930 memorandum is retrospective and procedural; use it to establish Greenman's long-term role, not to date each individual determination.
6. Keep taxonomic determination distinct from Rudolf's microscopic analytical work, exactly as Rudolf does in 1902.
7. A corrected name does not prove the physical glass object was altered.

## Working conclusion

The expedition's afterlife included a taxonomic audit. A decade after field collection, Goodale and Greenman were still implicated in sorting out names, while Rudolf defended the analytic work that belonged to his own craft. **This is another form of distributed manufacture: the glass body and the scientific identity were made, checked and repaired by partially different people on partially different clocks.**
