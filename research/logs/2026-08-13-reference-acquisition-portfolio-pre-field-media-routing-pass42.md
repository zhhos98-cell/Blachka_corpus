# Reference acquisition portfolio / pre-field media routing — pass 42 — 13 August 2026

## Result

Pass 42 returns to a 23 September 1889 Hosterwitz letter to Elizabeth and Mary Ware and reads it as an operational plan rather than as general background to the Harvard contract. The letter lays out several distinct ways of getting American botanical reference material into the workshop before Rudolf's 1892 expedition: seeds raised at Hosterwitz, difficult plants sent as roots through an intermediary route, roots and shrubs purchased from botanical/other gardens during the autumn, cut flowers and leaves obtained locally or abroad, and documentary lists/treatises used to define which species counted as relevant to an American flora.

This is stronger than saying that Harvard `sent plants`. The workshop had already assigned different biological materials to different acquisition and transport modes.

> **Before the field expedition, reference acquisition was already a portfolio problem. Seeds, roots, cut material, cultivated plants and documentary lists moved through different routes because they preserved different future uses.**

The 1892 journey therefore entered an existing system of **pre-field media routing** rather than replacing a single remote-supply channel.

## 1. Primary source: 23 September 1889

The letter survives in several repeated OCR witnesses, including source 030 pp. 292–293 and parallel copies in 034–036. It is addressed from Hosterwitz near Dresden to Elizabeth L. Ware and Mary L. Ware at West Rindge, New Hampshire.

The Blaschkas explain their arrangement with George Lincoln Goodale for obtaining `fresh material` needed to make the Harvard botanical collection as complete as possible.

The sequence is unusually explicit.

### A. Seed cultivation at Hosterwitz

For by far the greater part of open-air plants, they say it would be better to **raise them from seeds in their own garden**.

This turns transatlantic movement into deferred local observation:

`seed supplied → seed crosses distance → Hosterwitz cultivation → later target plant state → workshop observation/model work`.

The useful object in transit is therefore not the flower or mature plant but a compact biological future.

### B. Roots for difficult-to-raise groups

They immediately create an exception for plants too difficult to raise in Germany. The OCR gives examples approximately as Gentianaceae and some Ericaceae/related group; species-level normalization should wait on image checking. For these, Goodale had promised to supply **roots**, with the letter saying they would come `via Holland`.

This is a distinct transport choice:

`difficult seed route → switch propagule → root shipment → intermediary route via Holland → Hosterwitz cultivation/study`.

`via Holland` is direct OCR wording repeated across multiple witnesses and should be preserved literally until the exact commercial/botanical intermediary is identified.

### C. Autumn purchase of roots and shrubs

The Blaschkas say that during the preceding weeks they had also used the `autumnal occasion` to purchase **roots and shrubs of American plants** from a botanical garden and other gardens at reasonable prices.

Multiple OCR witnesses render the location in the continuation as `Sweden`. This geographical reading is suspicious in context and has not been normalized. The safe evidential content is that autumn seasonality was used for the purchase of living/rooted American material from garden suppliers.

### D. Cut flowers and leaves

For hothouse plants and trees, the workshop says **cut flowers and leaves for study** could be obtained `here and abroad`.

This is another acquisition mode because the desired future affordance differs. Such material need not survive as a living organism; it needs to arrive in a state useful for immediate observation.

### E. Documentary list/treatise

The same letter says that some species relevant to the American flora were also European wild plants, and that Goodale promised a **list or treatise** by which the Blaschkas could determine which species were to be included/recognized in the American Flora.

Thus documents governed the material search space. The workshop could obtain a plant locally only after documentary information established that the form mattered to the American collection.

## 2. The acquisition system was already media-specific

The letter supports a compact pre-1892 acquisition matrix:

| desired reference state | transport/acquisition form | post-arrival operation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| open-air species feasible from seed | seed | raise in Hosterwitz garden |
| difficult-to-raise species | root / living propagule | establish/cultivate locally |
| seasonally purchasable American plants | roots / shrubs from gardens | local cultivation and study |
| hothouse/tree material where whole-plant transport is unnecessary | cut flowers / leaves | immediate study |
| collection/floristic relevance | list / treatise | select/identify what material is worth acquiring |

This is an archival mechanism, not a retrospective analytical imposition: the actors themselves distinguish seeds, roots, shrubs, cut flowers/leaves and documentary aids according to practical difficulty and availability.

Working term: **acquisition-mode assignment** — choosing the form in which a desired botanical reference should move before transport begins.

---

## 3. A fifth clock: biological procurement before shipment

Pass 40 added a nursery/dispatch clock. The 1889 letter shows that this clock begins even earlier with a decision about **which propagule or material state is worth procuring**.

A fuller pre-workshop sequence can now be written:

`collection/floristic need`
→ `acquisition-mode assignment`
→ `supplier/garden/Harvard source`
→ `biological dispatch condition / season`
→ `transport route`
→ `Hosterwitz receipt`
→ `cultivation or immediate study`
→ `target biological state`
→ `modelling decision`.

This helps explain why geographical access and temporal access repeatedly separate in later sources. A plant can be `available in America` yet still require a seed season, root season, flowering season, cultivation interval or documentary determination before it becomes reference-ready.

## 4. Relation to the 1890 Gillett list and 1891 Fritillaria

The 1889 portfolio makes the later Southwick evidence less isolated.

By February 1890 the workshop refers to a previously sent selection/list of American plants. By 1890–91 Gillett & Horsford supplied living western material, including the California Fritillaria bulb that flowered at Hosterwitz and entered the July 1891 model consignment under taxonomic uncertainty. By June 1892 Rudolf remembered Gillett as someone who had sent catalogues and Cypripedium plants and planned to discuss further seed/plant acquisition.

The sequence now looks institutional rather than episodic:

`1889 multi-route acquisition design`
→ `1890 portable American-plant selection/list`
→ `1890–91 nursery supply / cultivation at Hosterwitz`
→ `1891 model production from remotely supplied living material`
→ `1892 field trip plus intended renewal of supplier relationships`.

The field expedition therefore supplemented a mature acquisition portfolio.

## 5. The documentary route is structurally important

Pass 32 described `collection-state media`: syllabi, desiderata and lists that encode gaps/redundancies. The 1889 list/treatise is an earlier form of the same general operation, although its exact content is floristic rather than a proven Harvard inventory.

The distinction should remain:

- plant reference media tell the workshop about morphology/colour/structure;
- floristic/collection-state documents tell the workshop **which plants count as relevant targets**;
- commercial catalogues tell the workshop **what can be procured and in what form**.

These documentary objects can alter material movement without themselves representing the plant in the same way a drawing or specimen does.

---

## 6. Stronger interpretation of `hitherto inaccessible`

The December 1891 phrase about American forms `hitherto inaccessible to us` becomes still narrower after this letter.

By September 1889 the Blaschkas already expected to obtain American plants through:

- seed cultivation;
- roots via an intermediary route;
- purchases from gardens;
- cut material from local/foreign sources;
- documentary aids supplied by Goodale.

Thus `inaccessible` cannot plausibly mean `American botanical material as such cannot reach Hosterwitz`. It points to the residual cases that this portfolio did not solve well: particular biological states, direct habitat relations, colour/form that did not survive supply, difficult taxa, uncertain identities or forms not present in cultivation/commercial networks.

## 7. Source-critical guards

1. The continuation OCR repeatedly reads the garden location as **`Sweden`**. Do not silently change it to Dresden, Germany, Saxony or another geography without image-level confirmation.
2. The family names around the difficult-to-raise exceptions are OCR-unstable (`Gentianaceae`, `Ericae`/related forms). Preserve the group-level point unless the manuscript is checked.
3. `via Holland` proves a stated route/intermediary geography, not the identity of a specific nursery, carrier or broker.
4. The letter describes a plan and some recent purchases. Do not assume every promised seed/root packet was subsequently dispatched or survived.
5. `here and abroad` for cut flowers/leaves establishes broad sourcing capacity, not a specific transaction.
6. The promised list/treatise should not yet be equated with the 1890 American-plant selection list or the later 1902/1903 Engler/desiderata documents.
7. Acquisition mode should remain separate from later model outcome. Seed/root/cut access does not imply that a model was ultimately made.

---

## 8. Article consequence

This letter gives the prehistory of the 1892 journey a concrete operational paragraph. The contrast is no longer `remote supply versus fieldwork`. Remote supply itself was heterogeneous and biologically designed.

Useful formulation:

> **Before Rudolf travelled, the workshop was already deciding how a plant should travel: as seed when future growth could be deferred to Hosterwitz, as root when germination was unreliable, as cut material when immediate morphology was enough, and as information when the first problem was knowing what to seek.**

A shorter version:

> **The first transport decision was often a media decision.**
