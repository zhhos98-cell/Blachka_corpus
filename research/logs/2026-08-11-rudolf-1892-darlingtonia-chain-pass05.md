## Why Darlingtonia matters

`Darlingtonia californica` is now the clearest test of the claim that Rudolf Blaschka's 1892 journey worked as a reference-production system rather than as a simple collecting itinerary. The chain contains a pre-existing commission desire, a costly route decision, remote communication with Cambridge, an explicit cancellation of the habitat excursion, a cultivated institutional encounter, several forms of reference capture, a surviving preparatory-drawing record, and a named Harvard glass model. Crucially, the links show that the plant's habitat, the place of observation, and the place of manufacture did not have to coincide.

Operationally the chain can now be written:

`Goodale priority → costed Shasta proposal → telegram / remote decision → Shasta cancelled → Colorado route continues → St Louis cultivated specimen → colour sketch + alcohol specimen → preparatory drawing record 133264 → Hosterwitz joint manufacture / finishing → Harvard model 444`

The chain is therefore closed at the level of **route decision, taxon, reference-capture event, archival drawing identifier, and glass-model identifier**, but not yet at the level of **one uniquely dated source object proving that drawing 133264 directly generated model 444**. That distinction should remain explicit.

## The important epistemic result

The St Louis episode now does more analytical work than when Shasta was treated as a possible visited site. A plant whose natural habitat generated a four-day, $70 route proposal was deliberately *not* pursued after remote consultation with Goodale. Days later, the same desired taxon became epistemically usable in St Louis because a botanical garden supplied a flowering specimen, identification context, a workroom, and preservable access. The relevant geography is therefore a chain of substitution, not a line from habitat to workshop: habitat was proposed, rejected as a trip, and functionally replaced by cultivated institutional material.

The strongest formulation is: Darlingtonia entered the expedition as a desired but logistically expensive target; its pursuit became a problem of time, money and telegraphic authority; Goodale's remote reply closed one possible field route; a botanical garden later converted the target into an observable and preservable specimen; the taxon survives in the preparatory-drawing archive; and Rudolf later identifies the Harvard glass object as Model 444. What remains to prove is the exact genealogical relation among the 1892 St Louis colour sketch, drawing 133264, and Model 444.

## Shasta: contradiction resolved as translation/source problem

The 18 May letter records Shasta as a proposal. The Colorado Springs follow-up is decisive: Rudolf states that the telegraphic reply said they should **not** go to Shasta (`BLA-D00874`, `017:228`). His account of the westbound-to-Colorado rail journey in `BLA-D00873`, `017:227`, beginning after Oakland and running via Sacramento, Sierra Nevada, Nevada, Utah and the Rockies, is also consistent with continuing east rather than taking a Shasta detour.

This exposes a real source-critical problem in `BLA-D00896`, `017:251`. Its English translation says that Darlingtonia was the plant “because of which we travelled to the Shasta Mountains.” Read against the explicit telegram and subsequent route narrative, that clause cannot safely be used as evidence that the Shasta trip occurred. It is either a translation/OCR problem or a retrospective phrasing that needs the German original checked. For the itinerary layer the conservative result is now stronger than before: **Shasta = proposed target / cancelled leg, not a visited node, unless a new primary source overturns the telegram evidence.**

This is methodologically useful for the paper. The very plant that seemed to require geographic presence demonstrates the opposite mechanism: knowledge production could be rerouted by communication and institutional substitution.

## A second result: fieldwork and Hosterwitz were temporally concurrent

The 7 Aug 1900 attribution letter contains a retrospective quotation from one of Leopold's letters written while Rudolf was away. OCR reads the date poorly as something like “March 30. 1892”; Rudolf translates Leopold as having written to Goodale that he was continuing work on plants “as per possibility” and that when his son returned “they can get finished” (`BLA-D01746`, `020:275`). The exact date and original letter still need recovery, so this is not yet a fully closed citation. Even in this guarded form it supplies an important research lead: the American trip and the Hosterwitz studio may have been operating simultaneously, with Leopold continuing the glass-work while Rudolf produced references abroad, and finishing explicitly deferred to Rudolf's return.

That would make the “distributed workshop” argument stronger. Distribution would describe not only Cameron, Ganong, botanical gardens, patrons and postal supply around the traveller, but also contemporaneous division of labour across the Atlantic. The field route and the workshop queue would be two synchronous parts of one production system.

## Contribution relative to the Corning narrative

The novelty threshold is now clearer. Existing scholarship already narrates the trip and connects field study to later glass flowers. The added value here is to reconstruct a source-critical, multi-medium provenance chain and to retain the frictions inside it: route choice, cost, telegraphy, cancelled travel, cultivated substitution, preservation, archival date uncertainty, joint artisanal labour, and asynchronous production. Granularity matters because it exposes transformations and substitutions that a narrative itinerary compresses. In the Darlingtonia case, the failed/cancelled habitat visit is itself productive evidence: the reference system worked precisely because observation could be detached from the expected place. The paper-level object is the **reference chain**, not the travel diary by itself.
