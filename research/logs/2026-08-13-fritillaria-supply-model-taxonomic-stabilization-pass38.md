# Fritillaria supply → model → taxonomic stabilization — pass 38 — 13 August 2026

## Result

Pass 38 closes a pre-1892 microhistory one step further. The 19 July 1891 workshop letter does not merely show that Gillett & Horsford had supplied a California bulb to Hosterwitz. It records a live identification problem: the Blaschkas called the plant *Fritillaria recurva*, explicitly doubted that identification because the flower colour did not match the described brilliant crimson form, made the model anyway because the specimen was morphologically distinctive, and expected Harvard to recognize the plant once the model arrived.

The annotated Harvard shipment list appears to preserve the next taxonomic state. In the position that can be reconstructed as model **237**, the list reads **`Fritillaria parviflora, Torr.`**. Modern taxonomic calibration indicates that *Fritillaria parviflora* Torr. is a historical Californian name now associated with *Fritillaria micrantha* A.Heller, while *Fritillaria recurva* Benth. remains a distinct accepted species. The historical sequence therefore looks less like a spelling correction than a genuine re-identification or taxonomic stabilization after the workshop had already recognized a mismatch.

The safest formulation is:

> **A living California bulb crossed the Atlantic with one provisional identity, flowered and became glass under acknowledged doubt, and appears in the Harvard shipment record under another Californian name. Taxonomic closure could therefore occur after material access and after modelling had already begun.**

The actor and exact date of the re-identification remain open. The surviving OCR does not justify assigning the change specifically to Goodale, Watson, or another Harvard botanist.

## 1. Direct workshop evidence: 19 July 1891

`BLA-D01412 / 018:283`, Hosterwitz near Dresden, 19 July 1891, reports that the current consignment of **60 plant models and 88 details** is finished and that the enclosed list will accompany the models after the final boxes and cases are ready.

The first species singled out for comment is described as:

- `Fritillaria recurva`;
- a **California bulb sent the previous year by Gillett & Horsford, Southwick, Massachusetts**;
- apparently inconsistent with the true species description because the expected sepals were brilliant crimson;
- the Hosterwitz plant instead had a **greenish-yellow, brown-dotted** flower and characteristic incurved sepals;
- despite the mismatch, the Blaschkas judged the plant `worth to be made`;
- lacking a suitable manual of western United States plants, they expected the recipient to recognize the plant after seeing the model.

This gives a complete pre-dispatch sequence:

`California living bulb → Southwick nursery/intermediary → transatlantic supply → cultivation/flowering at Hosterwitz → comparison with literature → identification mismatch recognized → model made under provisional name → Harvard expected to stabilize identification after receipt`.

The important distinction is between **material usability** and **taxonomic closure**. The plant was good enough to model before its name was secure.

## 2. Shipment-boundary reconstruction: 192–251 = 60 models

The Harvard annotated shipment-list OCR can be aligned across overlapping scans/variants.

A preceding page contains model numbers 194 onward; another gives the sequence through the 220s and 230s; a following page shows **251. Trillium grandiflorum**, immediately followed by the heading **`6th Shipment, January 1892`** beginning with model 252.

The 19 July 1891 workshop letter independently says the current consignment contains **exactly 60 plant models**.

The interval:

`192–251 inclusive = 60 models`

therefore closes the July 1891 consignment / reconstructed fifth-shipment block with very high confidence.

This is useful because the list OCR loses the global number column in one of the overlapping scans, but the preserved neighbouring numbers let the sequence be restored without inventing a new count.

## 4. Historical taxonomic change is substantive

Modern taxonomic calibration matters here because the historical names are easy to conflate.

- *Fritillaria recurva* Benth. remains an accepted western North American species.
- *Fritillaria parviflora* Torr. is a historical Californian name now treated under *Fritillaria micrantha* A.Heller in modern taxonomic databases.
- The authority **Torr.** is essential. *Fritillaria parviflora* Mart. is a different Eurasian name and should never be merged with the Californian Torr. usage.

Thus the annotated-list identity should not be normalized as if it were merely another spelling of *recurva*. The historical record points toward a real change of taxonomic placement.

The flower description is also suggestive. The workshop's greenish-yellow/brown-dotted plant fit their own reason for doubting brilliant-crimson *recurva*, while historical descriptions of the Californian *parviflora* / modern *micrantha* complex are compatible with brownish-purple or greenish flowers. This morphological fit strengthens the re-identification hypothesis, although it remains supporting calibration rather than direct proof of who made the determination.

## 5. Distributed taxonomic labour before the 1892 expedition

This microhistory sharpens pass 36. The remote-supply ecology did not only move plants.

It distributed different operations across institutions:

`Gillett & Horsford` → procurement / living-material supply

`Hosterwitz workshop` → cultivation / observation / comparison / modelling / recognition of mismatch

`Harvard-side botanical system` → expected determination and later stabilized nomenclature in the shipment record

The sequence is especially valuable because the workshop openly documents uncertainty. We do not have to infer taxonomic instability from later synonymy alone.

A useful mechanism term is **post-material taxonomic stabilization**: a reference can already have crossed the ocean, flowered, been judged morphologically useful, and been translated into glass while its institutional name remains provisional.

This is a pre-1892 example of the same broader taxonomic latency later visible in the American journey material.

## 6. Source-critical guards

1. **Model 237 is reconstructed from overlapping shipment-list OCR**, not presently visible as a single clean `237 Fritillaria` line.
2. The annotated shipment list does not yet tell us whether `parviflora` was written by the Blaschkas before dispatch, added by Goodale or another Harvard botanist after receipt, or normalized by a later cataloguer. Handwriting/image layers must be inspected.
3. Do not attribute the determination to Goodale merely because the workshop says `certainly you will know it easily`.
4. Do not treat *F. parviflora* Torr. as a synonym of *F. recurva*.
5. Do not silently replace the historical name *F. parviflora* Torr. with modern *F. micrantha* in quotations or historical tables. Preserve both historical and current taxonomic states.
6. Do not confuse *F. parviflora* Torr. with *F. parviflora* Mart.; the authorities refer to different taxa/name histories.
7. The modern morphological fit supports the re-identification hypothesis but does not prove the exact specimen/model lineage independently of the shipment-list position.

## 7. Consequence for the article

This case gives the pre-expedition supply ecology a particularly clean object-level example. It shows why the 1892 journey should not be narrated as a move from second-hand remote material to direct, fully identified observation. Before the journey, living American material already reached the workshop and could be observed in flower. The remaining friction could be taxonomic rather than simply geographic.

A concise formulation worth retaining:

> **Access could precede identification. The bulb reached Hosterwitz alive, the flower became glass, and the name remained mobile.**

A more technical version:

> **The reference chain transported biological material and taxonomic uncertainty together; nomenclatural stabilization could occur downstream of cultivation and modelling.**
