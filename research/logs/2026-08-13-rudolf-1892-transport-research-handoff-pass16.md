# Rudolf Blaschka 1892 transport research handoff — pass 16 — 13 August 2026

## Purpose

Freeze the current research state before the next conversation. This handoff consolidates passes 09–15 without replacing their source-critical detail. It records what is now closed, what remains inferential, which public-map changes have already been made, and the exact priorities for the next research pass.

The project has moved well beyond a person-itinerary history. The current object is a **multiplex transport system** connecting a split workshop, field reference work, finished glass, preserved and living botanical material, books, papers, brokers, customs, museum handling, settlement and exhibition installation.

The strongest current first-order formulation is:

> **1892 split the Blaschka workshop without stopping production. Transport kept the split workshop coupled by converting plants, models, papers and references into differently movable, handleable, inspectable, certifiable, countable and settleable forms. Later shipments aggregated asynchronous production histories into apparently coherent consignments. Arrival did not end transport: customs release, accounting, installation, vibration, repair and remounting could continue the object's mobility history.**

A concise alternative for prose development is:

> **Transport was not what happened after representation. Transport requirements helped determine the forms in which representation could exist.**

## 3. Person route and cargo route are genuinely different regimes

The strongest opening scene remains the split movement out of the same workshop.

### Cargo regime

`Hosterwitz → German railway freight → Bremen forwarder → Elbe → New York → in bond → Boston/Harvard receiving chain`.

### Rudolf regime

`Hosterwitz/Bremen → passenger documents → Saale/passenger steamship → Sandy Hook/Hoboken passenger customs → Ganong → hackney carriage → New York railway station → night train → Boston/Cambridge`.

The Bremen agent Ehrhorn, Emden & Mayer appears on both sides, handling freight and Rudolf's travel paperwork, but cargo and person remain institutionally distinct transport regimes.

Rudolf says the Elbe carrying the five cases and his Saale reached New York on the same day, cargo in the morning and Rudolf in the evening. A later passenger-list index creates a one-day discrepancy. Keep Rudolf's contemporaneous claim and the external discrepancy both visible until vessel records close the point.

## 4. Receiving-side handling: distributed expertise, not maker-only knowledge

BLA-D00695 / 017:49 remains one of the best primary documents in the whole case.

In the same letter:

- Rudolf sends roughly 29 species of Cambridge Botanic Garden seeds toward Hosterwitz as `sample without value`, with sowing/handling marks;
- the five glass-model cases arrive at Harvard;
- the cases are lifted upstairs;
- a museum porter/houseboy removes outer packing;
- Goodale opens a box himself and removes Datura arborea;
- Rudolf praises Goodale's careful unpacking;
- further opening waits on customs inspection.

A few days later Snow sends Capt. Wales of the Custom House; about eight specimens are opened and reported in excellent condition.

Interpretive correction: Harvard already possessed receiving-side handling skill. The safest formulation is **distributed handling knowledge**, not a one-way transfer of maker expertise from Rudolf.

## 5. Split workshop: Rudolf travelled while Leopold manufactured

The annotated list states that models **312–322** were made by Leopold Blaschka in the first half of 1892.

Rudolf's 1900 retrospective independently recalls the eleven models his father prepared from January to July 1892. Travel correspondence also shows Rudolf receiving reports about work at home while he was in the western United States.

Thus 1892 did not simply make the workshop mobile. It **split the workshop**:

- Rudolf: travelling observation, drawing, microscopy, preservation, repair, collection and future-supply relations;
- Leopold: continuing manufacture at Hosterwitz.

The eleven 312–322 models were made in 1892 but enter the February 1893 seventh shipment. This directly separates production clock from shipment clock.

## 6. Shipment as aggregation technology

The later shipment sequence now shows that a shipment is not a production-time unit.

### 7th shipment, February 1893

Model range: **312–373**.

It combines:

- 312–322, stationary Leopold production from first-half 1892;
- a substantial cryptogamic/scientific block;
- at least `a few` American plants collected on the 1892 journey, according to Goodale.

Specific American model numbers in this shipment remain partly unresolved. Abronia umbellata, model 345, is currently one of the strongest candidates because the species is directly observed in the 1892 western correspondence and Corning holds a preparatory drawing dated `1892 or 1895`.

### 8th shipment, July/August 1893

Ordinary plant block: **374–410 = 37 models**.

The 18 July 1893 letter partitions the 37 into:

- 20 after Jamaica studies;
- 15 from the West;
- 2 from garden material.

The numbered sequence produces an unusually exact fit:

- 374–393 = 20;
- 394–408 = 15;
- 409–410 = 2.

Named anchors support the reconstruction: Tribulus and Passiflora murucuja in the Jamaica block; Rosa woodsii, Rosa gymnocarpa and Viola in the western block.

Status: high-confidence inferred block, not yet a printed regional subdivision in the OCR. Inspect original list images/model labels before publication.

Models 411–423 form a scientific/cryptogamic/anatomical block in the same shipment.

### 9th shipment, January 1894

Boundary now corrected and closed:

**424–473 inclusive = exactly 50 models.**

Model 474 begins the tenth shipment.

The December 1893 workshop letter describes the January consignment as 50 species, overwhelmingly based on Jamaica and western material/studies except for a few stated exceptions.

### 10th shipment, August 1894

**474–523 inclusive = 50 models.**

Physical cases: **I.B.366–369**, four cases, sent toward Bremen.

Shipment size itself became negotiable. Goodale later proposed that 30 or 40 models would suffice for the next instalment because production difficulty and delay were pushing the makers beyond their capacity. The Blaschkas explicitly welcomed permission to reduce the number.

## 7. Strong object biographies

### Model 290 — Datura arborea

Model 290 belongs to the January 1892 sixth shipment and is the Datura removed during Harvard unpacking in February 1892.

Weeks later Rudolf describes living Datura arborea blossoms in Jamaica.

Chronological value:

`finished representation → transatlantic shipment → Harvard unpacking → later living encounter`.

This is an anti-linear control against any general claim that the expedition uniformly followed `field plant → glass model`.

Rudolf's 1900 maker account says Leopold's hand predominated in the glass shaping of models 290–291 while Rudolf states that joint-period models passed through his painting/finishing work. Do not call model 290 solely Leopold's model.

### Model 433 — Hoffmannseggia

This is the strongest current specimen/model divergence case.

Harvard University Herbaria preserves GH 01676890:

- Hoffmannseggia glauca;
- Arizona, Tempe?;
- collectors W. F. Ganong & R. Blaschka;
- April 1892.

The glass-flower sequence later contains model 433 under historical Hoffmannseggia synonymy in the January 1894 shipment. Rudolf's 1900 authorship letter lists 433 among models whose main construction he claimed as his own, while analytical details were generally Leopold's share.

Working object history:

`living Arizona plant → field encounter/collection → herbarium specimen retained at Harvard`

and separately:

`field reference lineage → Hosterwitz manufacture → model 433 → January 1894 shipment → Harvard`.

Key concept: **representational divergence**. The herbarium sheet and glass model coexist as different descendants of the same field encounter/reference lineage. They are not a simple before/after replacement chain.

Publication guard: inspect the original digitized model-433 label and the original specimen sheet/label before making a fully closed one-to-one modelling-source claim.

## 8. Selective and partial reassembly

Harvard's finding aid states that more than 300 herbarium specimens from the 1892 and 1895 expeditions remained at Harvard after Rudolf returned to Germany.

This corrects any model in which all field references eventually reunite at Hosterwitz.

The proper formulation is **selective / partial reassembly**:

- some specimens remain Harvard objects;
- some seeds and bulbs travel;
- some living material travels;
- some material is preserved in alcohol or dried;
- some drawings travel with Rudolf;
- some reference books are later purchased in Cambridge and sent separately;
- some field encounters become future-supply relations rather than immediate specimens.

---

## 9. Transport medium preserves different future affordances

The Agua Caliente / western correspondence supplies the strongest direct mechanism.

Ganong handles plants in glass containers with alcohol and water and as dried specimens. Boxes of material are staged at Cambridge.

Rudolf then says cacti are better received **alive** than in alcohol because `we can grow the flower ourselves`.

This gives a direct causal sequence:

`plant → decide what future property must remain available → choose transport/preservation medium → transport/stage → later observation/cultivation/modelling`.

Transportability is therefore not generic portability. Different mobile states preserve different future operations.

## 10. Borrowed commercial infrastructure: Boston Fruit Company in Jamaica

Rudolf's Jamaica travel was embedded in the Boston Fruit Company fruit-steamer network.

Direct correspondence shows:

- outbound passage on Bowden of the Boston Fruit Company;
- coastal delay because the steamer's banana work required additional calls;
- Boston Fruit Company personnel helping with lodging, ground transport and postal communication when scientific luggage/glass jars could not simply move by mail coach;
- return passage on Albert Dumois inside a cargo-collection loop that had to gather fruit before the Boston crossing.

This supports the narrow claim:

> **The scientific itinerary was partly produced by the cargo itinerary.**

Do not generalize this to `commerce enabled science`; that historiographical ground is already occupied.

High-priority counterpart archive: Baker Library, Boston Fruit Company records, including the 1891–1894 letterpress copybook and 1892–1898 private ledger.

## 11. Dispatch clock: finished is not dispatchable

The 18 July 1893 letter says the models are effectively ready except mounting, yet the exact shipping date remains uncertain because packing boxes must be ordered at the last moment and depend on the crate maker.

The same sequence explains that most models are fixed on glass panes because otherwise those fragile objects cannot be safely handled.

Thus:

**representationally finished ≠ logistically ready**.

Dispatch requires another transformation:

`finished glass model → mounted/handleable assemblage → crateable/insurable/certifiable consignment`.

The later 1930 transportation method expands the same logic into temporary supports, boxes, tissue, wooden cases, straw/burlap, customs opening and museum remounting.

## 12. Value and accounting

The 1890 contract specifies:

- 8,800 marks/year;
- two half-year instalments of 4,400 marks;
- two shipments annually;
- Harvard Botanical Department to defray expenses from place of manufacture to Cambridge, including insurance and consular certificate.

The August 1894 workshop account separately lists:

- Modelle — 4240 M;
- four cases — 50 M;
- cartons — 55 M;
- packing labour — 30.30 M;
- transport to Dresden — 11.50 M;
- insurance to Bremen — 2.50 M;
- consular certification — 10.70 M;
- total — 4400 M.

Do not equate 4240 with a simple production price or 4400 with a simple sale price. The coincidence with the half-year contractual instalment is striking but the source genre remains unresolved.

Safe finding: model value, cases, cartons, labour, local transport, insurance and consular documentation are explicit and separable accounting categories.

## 13. E. A. Snow: receiving-side infrastructure and procedural memory

Snow is now a longitudinal actor.

### 1892

Papers for U.B.346–350 are turned over to Snow. Snow sends Capt. Wales for Harvard-side customs inspection.

### March 1893

Goodale records a **$106.97** Snow bill whose substantially secure categories include:

- railroad bills in Germany;
- freight by the Ocean and the Sound Steamers;
- customs expenses;
- carriage for the inspector;
- Snow commission.

Interpretive result: **invoice as retrospective route map**. A Boston-side bill recompresses a geographically distributed multimodal chain after the movements occur.

Guard: the Sound-steamer evidence applies to this 1893 settled shipment only. Do not project it backward onto U.B.346–350.

### September 1893

Snow's bill covers freight/express from Hosterwitz to Cambridge plus inspector expenses and carries forward an omitted carriage cost from Col. Wales's previous visit. Goodale sends the memoranda to Ware `for preservation`.

Address: **42 Summer Street, Boston**.

Interpretive result: physical arrival and administrative closure have different clocks; accounts can preserve inter-shipment memory.

### 1906

Goodale says Snow was formerly a Custom-house Broker on State Street, was no longer in business, and `always did our work well`. Harvard has to hunt him up because incoming cases are still consigned to him.

### 1923

Rudolf still instructs R.B.1–4 to go Bremen → New York → in bond to Boston, care of E. A. Snow, Customhouse broker and forwarding agent. Bremen agents later discover Snow `does no more exist`; another agent must be found and Rudolf associates the failure with delay.

Strongest formulation:

> **Transport infrastructure was also memory infrastructure.**

A durable route was partly a durable memory of whom to address, who handled the papers, who summoned the inspector and who settled the charges.

Snow's first name/corporate identity remains unresolved. Do not guess.

## 14. Settlement/accounting clock

Pass 15 adds a distinct post-arrival clock.

A shipment can be physically at Harvard while:

- customs authorization remains incomplete;
- broker bills have not yet arrived;
- omitted inspector carriage remains unsettled;
- a later invoice carries an earlier residual cost.

Thus `arrival` is not administrative closure.

The current clock architecture is:

1. production;
2. dispatch;
3. transport/clearance;
4. settlement/accounting;
5. reference;
6. shipment/counting;
7. installation/exhibition.

The stronger statement is now:

> **The same object inhabited different clocks.**

## 15. Chicago 1893: installation is another mobility regime

The Harvard botanical models at the World's Columbian Exposition provide the strongest post-arrival control.

Ganong initially reports that despite rough express-company treatment the models were set up with only limited/easily repairable breakage.

Goodale's 28 May sequence then describes a different failure after installation. The velvet intended to keep the specimens safe acted, in the OCR's unusually clear phrase, as a **`machine for moving them in all directions`**. The models became huddled and many broke through movement/vibration in the exhibition setting.

Goodale then:

- creates a work area;
- repairs fractures;
- remounts and relabels;
- recovers the case;
- performs a practical stress test before reopening the display.

Interpretive result:

`freight success → installation failure → renewed movement → breakage → repair → remounting → testing → display`.

Display is therefore another handling/mobility regime, not the stable endpoint of transport.

Source guard: exact wording for the vibration source in the OCR is corrupt. Image-level transcription of BLA-D00145, D00149 and D00150 is required before publication-level quotation.

## 16. Material affordance reversals

Two longitudinal controls now make the same point from different directions.

### Velvet, Chicago 1893

Intended to stabilize; instead becomes a mechanism of object movement and breakage.

### Straw, Harvard 1929

Mechanically protective packing material; simultaneously a regulatory/quarantine problem because plant material itself can trigger import restrictions.

Thus a material does not carry one stable transport property. Its affordance depends on the regime in which it is embedded.

## 18. Historiographical guard

The contribution is **not** any of the following:

- circulation matters;
- logistics mattered to science;
- commercial networks carried knowledge;
- objects changed status in movement;
- customs could damage scientific objects;
- intermediaries mattered;
- the workshop was mobile/distributed.

Existing scholarship already covers much of that terrain.

The stronger mechanism currently specific to this material is:

> **Blaschka model-making worked across distance by controlled decomposition and selective reassembly. Plants, models, papers and references were split into transport-specific forms, passed through regimes that temporarily reassigned authority over them, and later recombined into shipments, museum objects and archival records on different clocks.**
