Status: ACTIVE ANALYTICAL PASS. Parallel to `2026-08-11-rudolf-1892-sketchbook-railway-running-log.md`. This file changes the unit of analysis from itinerary stops to the production of usable references. No public-map claims should be upgraded from this note without source-level verification.

## Working revision: from collecting trip to reference-production system

The 1892 journey is best reconstructed as a mobile reference-production system. The travel route matters because it governs access, time, weather, cost and bodily presence, but travel itself is not the final analytical unit. At each node living plants are selected, identified, drawn, microscopically studied, preserved, dried, converted into seeds or bulbs, labelled, forwarded, cultivated, repaired against existing models, or deferred into later supply. The workshop therefore extends across temporary rooms, botanical gardens, hotels, railway and steamship connections, postal and telegraph networks, local botanists, patrons, Harvard staff and the Hosterwitz studio.

A provisional operational chain is:

`living plant / habitat / cultivated specimen → selection → drawing + colour record + microscopy → alcohol specimen / herbarium / seed / bulb / cutting → identification + label → forwarding / cultivation → Cambridge receiving and comparison → Hosterwitz reassembly → glass model`

Two recurrent fallback loops matter as much as the main chain:

`missed flowering season → local supplier / later postal supply → seed, bulb, cutting or fresh material → future study`

`distant or expensive target → cost + time + telegraph / institutional coordination → route decision or cultivated substitute`

This makes the expedition temporally longer than Rudolf's bodily presence in America. It also makes its epistemic geography different from its travel geography: a plant associated with a remote habitat can become usable knowledge in a botanical garden; a missed flower can re-enter the system weeks later through a supplier; a field observation can become a multi-year production queue after it reaches Hosterwitz.

## What this changes relative to the published Corning narrative

Susan M. Rossi-Wilcox and David Whitehouse's 2007 chapter already supplies a strong narrative of the 1892 field season. It already notes, among other things, the high species count, the usefulness of identified plants in St Louis, the Darlingtonia episode, the Cambridge repair phase, multi-year estimates of future work, and more than 250 sets of models subsequently associated with the 1892 season. It also publishes a direct drawing-to-model comparison for `Jacaranda filicifolia`. Therefore the value of the present reconstruction cannot be claimed as simply a more detailed itinerary or the discovery that travel fed later model making.

The added contribution is a change in analytical resolution and structure:

1. **Operations rather than places.** Each stop is decomposed into encounter, selection, drawing/microscopy, preservation, procurement, coordination, circulation, repair and reassembly. This permits comparison between Hope Garden, a San Diego hotel, St Louis and Cambridge even though they are different kinds of sites.
2. **Several routes instead of one route.** Rudolf's bodily route, material routes, information routes and prospective supply routes do not coincide. Seeds can move to Hosterwitz while Rudolf moves elsewhere; a bulb can move from San Diego to Cambridge after he has left; Goodale can influence a western route by telegram from Cambridge.
3. **Logistics as epistemic infrastructure.** Railway time, luggage limits, glass jars, workrooms, preservation liquids, hotel arrangements, procurement budgets, forwarding and telegraphy change which observations are possible and which references survive. The separate railway-clock log should therefore be treated as causal evidence when timing changes observation or work windows, not as itinerary ornament.
4. **Temporal buffering.** The fixed season of travel is repeatedly extended through later supply. Missed flowering is not simply a failed observation; it can trigger a supplier relation and future cultivation. The field season is converted into a queue of future workshop work.
5. **Source contradictions remain productive.** Quantities such as 106, 116, 200, 350+, 400 and 250+ model sets are not interchangeable. The archive also gives differing estimates of the number of future work-years. Instead of flattening these into one total, the project should track what each number actually counts and who produced it.
6. **Object-level closure is the next threshold.** The map currently shows operational flows. A publishable advance over narrative synthesis will be stronger once several individual plants can be followed across multiple media and institutions to a finished model, including failures and deferred supplies.

## Yield ontology: stop treating every number as “species collected”

The current corpus contains several superficially comparable numbers that refer to different operations or moments. We should create separate fields before doing any quantitative claim:

- `encountered_or_considered`
- `drawn`
- `microscopically_studied`
- `colour_recorded`
- `alcohol_preserved`
- `herbarium_dried`
- `seed_or_bulb_acquired`
- `sent_or_forwarded`
- `cultivation_attempted`
- `modelled`
- `model_set_produced`

The Jamaica 106/116 discrepancy is especially useful because it may expose a category difference rather than an error: completed studies, coloured drawings, species represented, and dried counterparts need to be tested separately. The later 200/400 estimates and the retrospective 350+ / 250+ figures belong to still different counting regimes.

## Strongest species-level test: Darlingtonia

Darlingtonia is now the best candidate for a full provenance chain because the archive contains several distinct decision points rather than a single drawing/model pair.

Current links:

1. Goodale identifies `Darlingtonia californica` as a desired plant before the western decision.
2. `BLA-D00856` records the proposed Shasta journey as a four-day, $70 problem and the decision to telegraph Goodale.
3. `BLA-D00896` records Darlingtonia in bloom at St Louis, followed immediately by a colour sketch and alcohol preservation.
4. The Rakow finding aid lists a `Darlingtonia californica` preparatory drawing, dated broadly `1892 or 1895`, identifier `133264`, box 16 folder 85.
5. The corresponding Harvard glass-model record, exact model number/date, and any surviving herbarium or alcohol specimen still need to be closed.

Critical caution: the current route reconstruction does **not** yet treat Shasta as a secure visited node. The English translation of the 31 May letter can be read as saying the plant was one for which they travelled to the Shasta Mountains, while the map pass deliberately withheld Shasta pending the German original/image. Corning's published narrative treats a Shasta search as having occurred. This is now a source-critical problem, not a detail to smooth over. The next pass should compare the German pages around `017:206–210` and `017:251` directly before changing the itinerary.

If this chain closes, it will do more analytical work than another itinerary refinement: it would show request → route/cost decision → remote communication → institutional access → visual record → preserved specimen → workshop return → glass object, with an explicit ambiguity about the habitat leg.

## Control and negative cases

`Jacaranda filicifolia` is useful as a control because the 2007 Corning chapter already publishes the Jamaica drawing next to the resulting glass model. It demonstrates that drawing-to-model continuity is real, while also showing why that continuity alone is not our novelty claim.

`Crinum` should be tested as an asynchronous-supply case. The 10 June Cambridge letter shows the bulb arriving from Orcutt after an earlier seed packet and explicitly links future orders to flowers Rudolf could not wait for in California. The question is whether any later drawing, cultivation record or model can be connected to this supply.

A Sequoia case may work as a productive failure: the desired flowering material was seasonally unavailable, local botanists were expected to supply material later, and the failed encounter therefore generated a different circulation strategy. A cactus case could provide the opposite pattern, where Rudolf personally collected living material and carried it through the route before forwarding.

## Labour topology to extract next

The trip should be modelled as a participant network with task-specific edges rather than a generic list of companions:

- Rudolf: selection, drawing, microscopy, colour recording, some collecting, later repair and workshop reassembly.
- Cameron: local access, specimen procurement, rum/alcohol preparation, herbarium drying, transport of jars/boxes.
- Ganong: field collecting, preservation, herbarium work, seed contribution, route discussion, telegraph proposal, institutional introductions.
- Goodale: commission design, duplicate filtering / receiving-side priorities, remote route authority, Harvard workspace and repair context.
- Wares: financing, patronage, social introductions and commission governance.
- Orcutt: commercial botanical supply, excursion coordination, bulbs/seeds, delayed post-field supply.
- Dewart: lodging coordination and workroom access at St Louis.
- local botanists / garden staff: identification, locality knowledge, future flower or seed supply.
- Leopold / Hosterwitz workshop: receiving, cultivation and conversion of distributed reference media into models.

This makes “distributed workshop” an empirically testable description: it means a division of labour, media, workspace, expense and handoff, not simply that many people helped.

## Provisional paper-level claim

A defensible formulation at this stage is: Rudolf Blaschka's 1892 journey did more than bring the maker into contact with American plants. It reorganised model-making as a distributed system for producing references. Plants moved between habitat, garden, hotel room, herbarium, alcohol jar, seed packet, patronage network and workshop; information moved by letter, label and telegraph; missed seasons were repaired by later supply; botanical institutions substituted identification and cultivated access for parts of wild collecting; and the resulting records were converted into several years of workshop labour. The historical object is therefore not only the journey or the glass model, but the chain that made a living, seasonal and geographically dispersed plant reproducible in Hosterwitz.
