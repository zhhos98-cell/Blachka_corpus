# Southwick phenological dispatch window — pass 41 — 13 August 2026

## Purpose

Pass 40 established that the Southwick firm later advertised packing methods capable of carrying small living-plant orders even to Europe. This pass moves the chronology back to 1890 and asks when the nursery itself said orders could actually be filled.

## Result

A searchable modern reprint of the **1890 Southwick Nurseries wholesale catalogue of North American perennials, new Tigridias, California bulbs, etc.** preserves a short but highly relevant order instruction. It says orders were filled **when the plants were ripe**, mostly from **September through December**, and asks that orders arrive early.

The original 1890 catalogue is independently verified by BHL/NAL metadata and survives as an 18-page digitized object (`southwicknurseri1890sout`; BHL bibliography 85446). The exact wording above is currently recovered through a modern facsimile/reprint excerpt rather than direct page-image transcription, so publication use should inspect the original image. The operative mechanism, however, is clear enough to register as a high-priority source claim pending image confirmation.

## 1. The nursery had its own biological dispatch clock

The instruction changes how the 1890 California-bulb shipment should be conceptualized. Southwick could not fill every living-plant order whenever a customer asked. Dispatch depended on the stock reaching a transportable biological state described commercially as `ripe`.

The working chain becomes:

`customer request / desideratum`
→ `nursery stock exists`
→ `wait for plant/bulb to reach dispatchable state`
→ `autumn dispatch window, mainly Sep–Dec`
→ `packing/carrier`
→ `long-distance transit`
→ `Hosterwitz cultivation`
→ `later flowering / observation / modelling`.

This adds a **nursery dispatch clock** between procurement and transport. It is distinct from the workshop's later production and shipment clocks.

## 2. Implication for the Blaschka Fritillaria episode

The Blaschkas wrote on 19 July 1891 that their California *Fritillaria recurva* bulb had been sent by Gillett & Horsford, Southwick, `last year`. The 1890 catalogue's stated dispatch practice means the firm's ordinary bulb/perennial trade was concentrated in an autumn window.

This does **not** prove that the particular Fritillaria left Southwick between September and December 1890. The individual invoice or letter is still missing. It does show that such a date range is a historically grounded **candidate dispatch window** rather than an arbitrary guess.

Use status:

- exact Fritillaria shipment date: `unresolved`;
- firm-level 1890 dispatch season: `Sep–Dec, mostly`, pending original-image confirmation;
- inference that Fritillaria probably followed that window: `plausible_not_proven`.

## 3. Transport begins before the parcel moves

The source reinforces a larger project pattern. Biological transport readiness precedes physical movement. For living reference material, an order can exist while the object is still not dispatchable.

Compare the emerging clocks:

- **field phenology clock** — when a flower/fruit/seed state becomes observable;
- **nursery ripeness/dispatch clock** — when commercial stock can be lifted/packed/sent;
- **transit clock** — when the parcel physically moves;
- **Hosterwitz cultivation clock** — whether transported material survives and later reaches the needed state;
- **taxonomic clock** — whether identity is stable enough for workshop use;
- **production clock** — when the reference is activated into glass.

The same California bulb can therefore be materially available in commerce but epistemically useful only months later after shipment and cultivation.

## 4. Stronger interpretation of remote supply before 1892

The pre-expedition supply ecology was not instantaneous mail-order access. It was seasonally gated. Harvard or the workshop could request a plant, but a nursery had to synchronize the request with stock biology before packing it for long-distance movement.

A concise formulation:

> **Remote access compressed geography without eliminating season. The nursery could move a California bulb to Saxony, but only after commercial horticulture had made it transportable, and Hosterwitz still had to make it observable again.**

This is more precise than saying that nursery commerce simply solved field access.

## 5. Source-critical guards

1. The `ripe / September–December / order early` wording is currently recovered from a searchable modern reprint excerpt; inspect the original 1890 scan before quotation in publication.
2. The 1890 BHL object is securely identified as the Southwick wholesale catalogue and explicitly includes `California bulbs` in the title.
3. Do not infer that every class of stock followed the same exact dispatch dates; the catalogue instruction is firm-level/general unless page context narrows it.
4. Do not convert the firm-level window into the exact Fritillaria shipment date.
5. Do not assume `ripe` means botanical fruit/seed ripeness; in nursery trade language it may mean sufficiently mature/dormant/ready for lifting and shipment. Preserve the source term until page context is read.
