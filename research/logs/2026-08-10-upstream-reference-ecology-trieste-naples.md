# Upstream reference ecology: Naples, Trieste, Weymouth and workshop production

Date: 10 August 2026

## 1. The source chain now begins before glass

The source survey no longer starts with orders for finished Blaschka models. Three distinct routes brought biological reference material into the workshop:

1. published natural-history illustrations, including Müller and Gosse;
2. preserved marine animals, including the 1877 Naples Zoological Station transaction;
3. living organisms shipped to Dresden from Trieste, Weymouth and other coasts.

Workshop drawings form a fourth layer that could translate or combine these inputs before glassmaking. The result is a reference ecology rather than a progression from indirect pictures to direct observation.

## 2. Naples: an exact 1877 preserved-specimen lead

Drew Harvell reports examining in the Stazione Zoologica Anton Dohrn archive a Leopold Blaschka letter dated 4 February 1877 specifying 41 preserved marine animals to be sent for constructing scientifically accurate replicas. Separate Blaschka scholarship cites Anton Dohrn archive material from March and November 1877. These documents have not yet been cross-walked, so request, fulfilment and follow-up remain open.

The ASZN is a strong retrieval environment: correspondence, general-affairs registers, copybooks and other administrative/scientific records survive, while the station's institutional history explicitly describes the sale and international dispatch of preserved biological preparations. The next task is therefore a narrow transaction reconstruction, not further discovery of Naples as a source.

## 3. Trieste: reciprocal circulation rather than a one-way supply chain

A direct Rudolf Blaschka workshop account in the local OCR corpus states that a room in the Dresden house contained seawater tanks with living animals sent partly from the Imperial Austrian Zoological Station at Trieste and partly from England/northern coasts, and that in this way the zoological model work improved.

University of Vienna collection research reconstructs the relation in greater detail. Live-animal shipments reached Dresden from 1878. Carl Claus and Franz Eilhard Schulze also ordered glass models. In 1880 Claus insisted that finished models should be routed to Vienna rather than directly to Trieste; Leopold resisted because he feared the quality of the live-animal shipments would suffer, and he had to seek alternative suppliers. By 1884 the relation had recovered: marine animals again arrived from Trieste, Claus ordered 38 models and then 77 more, with a probable final Vienna order of 21 in February 1887.

The same Vienna source describes the live cargo travelling in jars placed in padded baskets, sent express/urgent only when temperatures allowed, with robust specimens selected because many organisms could not survive the journey. The historical problem is therefore reciprocal: institutional routing of finished representations could affect access to the living bodies needed to make representations.

The Austrian State Archive supplies a controlled administrative route: Unterrichtsministerium Signatur 2A contains named series for Zoologische Station Triest (1873–1903 and later) and Zoologische Station Neapel (1874–1906 and later). A separate Schönbrunn Menagerie file dated 14 January 1878 thanks Eduard Graeffe at the Trieste station for animals sent, independently demonstrating a station-to-inland animal-dispatch function in the same decade.

## 4. Printed images did not disappear when living animals became available

Corning Museum of Glass identifies Leopold watercolors of Asterias species as direct copies from Otto Friedrich Müller's 1778 Zoologia danica. Corning further states that even after the workshop had its own aquarium, Leopold and Rudolf frequently continued to base drawings on Müller and Philip Henry Gosse. Australian Museum research also uses specific nineteenth-century illustrations to identify surviving models.

This prevents a simple narrative in which direct observation superseded book illustration. Images, preserved bodies and living animals remained different technical resources.

## 5. Production was also internally modular

Corning preserves forty labelled matchboxes of premade glass components. One box holds 197 parts; another holds several hundred. The Museum states that body parts were made ahead of time to streamline and standardize production and were selected and assembled only after an order arrived, using wire and glue before painting and finishing.

This changes how order dates should be read. An order could trigger assembly and customization from an existing stock of components rather than initiate all glassmaking from raw material.

Conservation provides an exact drawing-to-object example. Three 1885 sea-cucumber models, Blaschka Nrs. 282, 284 and 287, share segmented tubular construction with joins in nearly the same locations. A digitized schematic drawing for Nr. 284 shows three body sections whose join positions exactly match those inside the finished model. The reason for making the models in sections remains unresolved; Corning offers glass-tube limitations or production simplification only as possibilities.

## 6. Revised research chain

The source architecture now supports a longer and less linear sequence:

published image / preserved specimen / living animal → transport and survival → workshop drawing → premade component stock → order → assembly and finishing → packing → dealer/carrier/payment network → museum receipt → display/damage/repair.

Crucially, the arrows can reverse. Trieste is a documented case in which living animals moved inward while glass models moved outward through the same institutional relationship.
