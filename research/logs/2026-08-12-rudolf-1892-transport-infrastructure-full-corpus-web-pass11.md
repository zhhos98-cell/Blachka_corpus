# Rudolf 1892 transport infrastructure: full-corpus + GitHub + web calibration pass 11 — 12 August 2026

## Purpose and hold

This pass deliberately pauses further public-map feature work. The aim is to test whether the emerging person/object-route interpretation survives a broader evidential and historiographical audit, and to identify the mechanisms that make 1892 more than an unusually detailed itinerary.

The working question is no longer simply where Rudolf Blaschka went or where the five January cases went. It is:

> How did the Harvard botanical-model commission work at distance by separating people, finished models, repair materials, botanical references, instructions, documents and decisions into differently transportable packets, passing them through different legal and material regimes, and later reassembling them at Cambridge or Hosterwitz?

`Packet`, `packetization`, `handoff authority`, `latency`, and `controlled decomposition` are analytical terms used in this research pass. They are not actor categories unless a primary source independently uses corresponding language.

No additional public-map packets were added during this pass. The map structure created in pass 10 remains the current public implementation while the evidence is recalibrated.

## Result in one sentence

The transport interpretation survives the audit, but its strongest form is narrower than “logistics mattered” or “knowledge circulated”:

> **Blaschka model-making at distance depended on controlled decomposition across handoffs: heterogeneous parts of the work were made differently transportable, moved under different legal and material regimes, temporarily placed under different actors’ authority, and reassembled on different clocks; transport therefore selected media and altered object states rather than merely carrying finished knowledge.**

This formulation should be treated as the current analytical proposition, not as a quotation or settled historiographical label.

## I. January–February 1892: the decisive parallel experiment

### 1. One production schedule releases two different mobile entities

BLA-D00016, 20 January 1892, remains the cleanest opening document. Leopold and Rudolf announce that the following day they will send by railway freight five cases, U.B. 346–350, containing 60 plant models and 329 analytical details. They specify the Bremen forwarding agents, New York, and “in Bond to Boston.” They also state that finishing this, their largest consignment so far, delayed Rudolf’s own journey by several days.

This single document binds together but does not merge:

1. a finished-model freight packet;
2. Rudolf’s separately financed passenger movement;
3. the production-time decision that links their departures.

The historical advantage is unusually strong. The maker and the models share a workshop origin and production schedule, then enter different infrastructures. The cargo becomes numbered freight, shipping papers and bonded merchandise. Rudolf becomes a passenger with tickets, luggage, travel documents and a personal customs declaration. Their separation is not a retrospective analytical convenience; the workshop itself records that the consignment delayed the traveller.

### 2. The same forwarder handled cargo and maker through different services

The GitHub longitudinal register shows Ehrhorn, Emden & Mayer repeatedly in Blaschka records from at least 1891 to 1929. In 1891 the firm coordinates five large cases and shipping advice. In February 1892 Rudolf writes that the firm’s comptoir was adjacent to his Bremen hotel and that the family’s agent supplied his travel documents; the existing repository reconstruction identifies his Norddeutscher Lloyd first-class passage on *Saale*. Later records show the same firm advising on routes, insurance, consular paperwork, bonded movement and carrier choice.

The important point is functional, not merely prosopographical. A single commercial intermediary could interface with both passenger mobility and freight mobility while preserving the distinction between them. This supports treating the forwarder as a source of procedural and route knowledge, not as a generic “carrier.” The later longitudinal evidence also makes visible a form of commercial memory: the Blaschkas repeatedly returned to a firm that knew their preferred route, paperwork and aversion to repacking.

No dedicated public archival fonds for Ehrhorn, Emden & Mayer has yet been identified. This continuity is therefore grounded in Blaschka-side evidence and should not be embellished into a corporate history before Bremen commercial records are checked.

### 3. External shipping data corroborate a matching *Elbe* departure, but do not yet provide the bill of lading

Goodale’s receiving correspondence says that five cases of glass models had arrived by the *Elbe* and that he passed the papers to E. A. Snow. Rudolf later expressed surprise that “our five boxes,” which had left well before him, reached New York at approximately the same time as he did.

The Bremen emigration-ship departure database, a joint project based on material of the Bremen Chamber of Commerce and Staatsarchiv Bremen, lists Norddeutscher Lloyd’s *Elbe*, captain K. von Gössel, departing Bremen for the USA on **30 January 1892**, with 447 passengers. This fits the archive chronology: the cases were announced for rail dispatch on 21 January; Rudolf left Hosterwitz/Bremen later and sailed on *Saale* on 2 February; the Harvard correspondence identifies the five cases as arriving by *Elbe*.

Evidence guard: the matching 30 January *Elbe* sailing is a strong route/date corroboration. It is not yet the direct cargo record for U.B. 346–350. A bill of lading, freight manifest, forwarding ledger or customs bond would be needed to turn the matching sailing into a formally documented case-to-vessel identification.

### 4. “In Bond to Boston” is a legal-administrative route, not decorative shipping prose

The phrase in BLA-D00016 can now be placed in a specific U.S. customs regime. The U.S. Act of 10 June 1880, chapter 190, amended the statutes concerning the “immediate transportation of dutiable goods.” A near-contemporary Supreme Court explanation in *Seeberger v. Schweyer* (1894) states that goods imported at New York and destined for an authorized inland/destination port could, after prescribed entry and such examination as was necessary, be transported onward by designated bonded carriers; responsibility for safe delivery remained with those carriers, and appraisement occurred at the destination rather than the first-arrival port.

For this project, the consequence is conceptual and archival. “New York → Boston” should not be rendered as an ordinary freight segment. The cases were being kept inside a customs-controlled relation while physically moving between ports. The freight therefore acquired an administrative state that Rudolf’s body and baggage did not share.

The present map’s distinction between passenger customs, bonded freight and museum customs opening is therefore historically necessary, not interface over-complication.

### 5. NARA Record Group 36 is the correct external archival environment

The National Archives at Boston describes Record Group 36, Records of the U.S. Customs Service, as covering the enforcement of import/export rules, entrance and clearance of vessels, merchant-vessel documentation and related customs administration. The existing GitHub logistics register already identifies Boston/Charlestown customs records as extending across the Blaschka period and flags cargo manifests, warehousing and customs administration as relevant record types.

The next external archive request should therefore be framed around the transaction rather than the surname alone: February 1892, five cases U.B. 346–350, *Elbe*, New York immediate transportation/in-bond movement to Boston, E. A. Snow, Harvard/University Museum/Botanical Museum, and any associated Boston collector/broker/inspection entry. The target is a bond/entry/manifest/warehouse/collector chain, not an assumed surviving “Blaschka file.”

## II. Cambridge: receiving skill, customs authority and object-state change

### 6. Harvard already possessed receiving-side handling competence

BLA-D00695 / 017:49 is stronger than a generic “shipment arrived” note. In the same letter, Rudolf reports that the five boxes reached the museum, were brought to the third floor by the building’s lifting equipment, and underwent an initial handling sequence. The museum porter/house servant removed outer packing; Goodale unscrewed/opened a case and removed *Datura arborea*; Rudolf explicitly characterized Goodale’s unpacking as skilful/careful. Further opening waited for the customs inspector.

This rules out a simple model in which the maker carries otherwise absent embodied skill to Cambridge. Harvard already had handling knowledge distributed among receiving actors. Rudolf’s presence still matters, but the stronger historical object is **distributed handling competence plus distributed authority**.

A later Goodale letter records E. A. Snow sending Capt. Wales of the Custom House. About eight specimens were opened and found in excellent condition, while the remainder awaited further unpacking and the presence of Ganong or another helper. This is a sampled condition report. It does not justify a blanket “all five cases arrived without a scratch.”

### 7. Handoff authority can now be operationalized

The evidence permits a more precise use of “handoff.” At different moments, different actors acquire limited authority over the object:

- the workshop can number, pack and dispatch;
- the forwarder can route, document and book;
- the bonded carrier can possess the cases while remaining responsible to customs;
- customs can authorize, detain, inspect and require opening;
- the broker handles papers and clearance;
- museum staff can move and remove outer packing;
- Goodale/Ganong/Ware-side handlers can unpack and inspect;
- Rudolf can assess, repair or remake damaged components.

These authorities are partial and temporary. They do not form a neat transfer of ownership. For the article and map, “handoff authority” should therefore mean **temporary, evidence-based power to classify, route, open, handle, inspect, remount or repair a packet/object**.

### 8. A source-critical correction is now mandatory in the packing register

`sources/packing-feedback-unpacking-register.json` currently contains `PACK-1892-CUSTOMS-OPENING` with two assertions that should not be used publicly without reconstruction: it calls the five cases “L.B. 1–5” and summarizes their condition as “without even a scratch.” The direct 1892 evidence now audited supports U.B. 346–350 as the January case marks, identifies five boxes arriving, supports a good condition assessment for the first examined material, and supports a later customs sample of roughly eight items in excellent condition. It does not support the current derived register’s exact `L.B. 1–5` phrasing or whole-consignment scratch-free generalization from the evidence isolated so far.

Action: keep this event flagged as a derived record requiring correction before the next public data deployment. Do not silently rewrite the source while the exact record genealogy is still being reconstructed.

## III. Repair is a transport process too

### 9. Rudolf’s American journey was planned from the start as repair + fresh study

Rossi-Wilcox reproduces Goodale’s 15 April 1890 account of the renewed arrangement: the Blaschkas proposed that Rudolf make an American voyage in winter 1891–92 “in order to repair our broken models and make fresh studies.” Goodale would supply travel expenses. This matters because it prevents the repair episode from appearing as an accidental afterthought to a collecting expedition.

The person route from conception carried two tasks: new reference acquisition and intervention in a pre-existing, already damaged Harvard collection.

### 10. The repair workshop was itself decomposed into mailable materials

The 2 February 1892 letter/request is one of the most consequential transport documents in the corpus. Rudolf asks his father to send the workshop’s regular dry paints because suitable dry pigments were difficult/expensive to obtain in Boston. He specifies small amounts, separate small envelopes, heavier outer covers, and “sample without value” treatment; he also asks for other workshop materials/tools while noting that lacquer, turpentine and brushes can be sourced in Boston.

This changes the meaning of “maker mobility.” Rudolf’s embodied presence was insufficient by itself. To perform repair in Cambridge, selected elements of the Hosterwitz material workshop had to be detached, repackaged into postal form, and made to catch up with the travelling maker.

The 1892 system therefore includes at least three westbound movements before the major field-return traffic is considered:

- U.B. 346–350 as freight;
- Rudolf as passenger;
- a repair-material micro-packet as international post.

Each has different packaging, documentary language, risk and admissibility.

### 11. “Sample without value” aligns with an active UPU postal category

This phrase appears repeatedly enough in the correspondence to require institutional interpretation. In February–April 1892 the applicable international postal framework was still the 1878 Universal Postal Convention as modified by the Lisbon Additional Act of 1885, which entered into force on 1 April 1886. The Vienna convention of 1891 did not enter into force until 1 July 1892.

The 1878 convention explicitly recognizes international “samples of merchandise” as a postal category, requires them to be packaged so they can be readily examined, and excludes sample packets having saleable value. The Lisbon act continues the sample-of-merchandise category.

Inference, with guard: Rudolf’s wording “sample without value,” together with his instructions to divide dry pigments into small envelopes and heavier covers, strongly aligns with this international postal regime. The source currently establishes the actor’s wording and packaging plan; the UPU texts establish the available regulatory category. A surviving wrapper, postage mark, customs declaration or postal receipt is still needed before stating that a specific Rudolf/Leopold packet was formally accepted under a particular UPU tariff/category.

This is nonetheless analytically important. Repair knowledge was made transportable by becoming a legally/postally admissible packet.

### 12. June repair must be separated from U.B. 346–350

The older clustered backend event `CONS-1892-01` combines U.B. 346–350, Rudolf’s parallel journey, customs damage and June repair. That is too coarse.

Goodale’s 10 June letter says Rudolf had repaired nearly all the damaged models, with two especially bad examples to go back to Germany, and describes those two as coming from **“the first consignment”** and as having been damaged at the New York custom house. On 15 June Goodale praised the repairs and noted that some petals had even been remade in Cambridge using available apparatus.

Rossi-Wilcox likewise places severe customs damage in the **first** botanical shipment, when inspectors unpacked the new models hastily. By contrast, the February 1892 receiving sequence for U.B. 346–350 documents excellent condition in the material actually sampled/opened.

Current rule: the June repair event is an accumulated-collection repair episode, some of whose damage is directly attributed to the first consignment/New York customs. It must not be back-assigned to U.B. 346–350 without model/case-level evidence.

## IV. Jamaica shows the same mechanism at another scale

### 13. The field party travelled with an object ecology, not just “luggage”

BLA-D00704, Hope Garden, 6 March 1892, is unusually explicit about field logistics. Rudolf recounts coastal steamer travel after St Antonio: an expected short trip to Annotto Bay was extended because the vessel first called at another northern port to deliver bananas, then Port Maria, reaching Annotto Bay around midnight. A Boston Fruit Company employee helped secure accommodation. Rudolf worried about transporting the party’s copious luggage because the mail coach did not accept trunks; Cameron had brought still more boxes containing glass jars and related equipment, producing a substantial wagon load. A local clerk, Kennedy, assisted.

This is not generic travel colour. It demonstrates that the expedition’s epistemic capacity was physically carried in boxes, jars and luggage, and that its movement depended on infrastructures built for other purposes.

### 14. The Boston Fruit Company link is externally researchable

Harvard Business School’s Baker Library holds **Boston Fruit Company records, 1891–1901**, call number Mss:1 1891–1901 B747, three volumes. The finding aid says the surviving material is fragmentary but provides an operational/financial view of the company in the 1890s. It also confirms that the firm’s Jamaica operations were overseen by Lorenzo Dow Baker and that its ships moved bananas from Jamaica to U.S. ports including Boston and New York; the Boston offices/docks were at Long Wharf.

This is a particularly promising external archive target because the surviving collection begins in 1891, immediately before Rudolf’s trip, and the primary letter names a Boston Fruit Company employee as a local logistics helper. The research question should be narrow: can the 1892 volumes identify Annotto Bay/Port Antonio/Port Maria operations, personnel, agency arrangements, ships or financial entries that contextualize Kennedy/the unnamed company employee and the route Rudolf used? There is no present evidence that the three surviving volumes mention Rudolf.

### 15. Commodity logistics and scientific logistics intersect without becoming identical

The March letter gives a concrete version of a broader point: the field journey did not run on a bespoke scientific infrastructure. A coastal steamer’s banana-delivery obligations change the party’s arrival time; company personnel assist accommodation; mail-coach baggage rules force another transport solution; boxes and jars require wagon capacity.

The correct claim is therefore intersection and dependence, not appropriation of the whole banana trade into the Blaschka story. A commercial perishable-commodity network structured when and how a scientific-artisanal party and its apparatus could move.

## V. Transportability selected epistemic media

### 16. Living reference material was already a transport problem before the expedition

Rossi-Wilcox shows Goodale sending seeds, cuttings and plants to the Blaschkas over the years and reports that the workshop preferred living reference material. Goodale also considered whether dry herbarium specimens might permit a broader model programme. The difficulty of representing exotic plants that could not be obtained or cultivated locally helped motivate the American expedition.

This gives 1892 a deeper prehistory. The trip was one solution to a mismatch between preferred epistemic medium (living material) and long-distance availability.

### 17. Jamaica produced multiple portable derivatives of the same living encounter

The Jamaica correspondence and the Jamaican Botanical Department report show the field encounter being converted into several media: coloured drawing, microscopic observation/record, dried/herbarium material, preserved material, seeds and other living propagules. The official Jamaican report states that Blaschka produced accurate coloured drawings of 116 species and that Robert Cameron made dried collections of corresponding and additional plants for the herbarium.

The point is not that one medium always replaces another. It is that the field encounter is **decomposed across media with different transport properties and later uses**. Colour can travel in a drawing; morphology can travel in dried/preserved material; potential future living material can travel as seed/bulb; taxonomic and comparative context can travel in published references.

This is the strongest basis for saying transport selected media.

### 18. February Cambridge seeds and April Jamaica seeds must remain separate packets

The working ledger correctly distinguishes at least two eastbound seed events:

- February: roughly 29 species from Cambridge Botanic Garden, sent toward Hosterwitz with sowing-treatment instructions in the same letter that records the arrival/unpacking of the five Blaschka cases at Harvard;
- April: Jamaican seeds sorted after Rudolf’s return from Jamaica and posted toward Hosterwitz, including named taxa and cultivation comments.

These should not be merged into a generic “seeds to Dresden” flow. Their provenance, timing, taxonomic context and relation to the field trip differ.

The February letter is especially valuable because one documentary page captures simultaneous opposite-direction traffic: finished glass arriving at Cambridge while botanical propagules leave Cambridge for Hosterwitz.

### 19. Return to Hosterwitz did not reunify the workshop immediately

Rudolf reached Hosterwitz on 30 June. In the 5 July letter he says he has resumed work but will make further selections when the preserved specimens acquired during the journey arrive. Human return therefore precedes material return.

Additional post-return correspondence shows reference literature continuing to be acquired/forwarded and later American plants returning to Harvard in glass form. This is a measurable latency problem, not merely a metaphor of circulation: the person clock, preserved-specimen clock, book/reference clock and modelling/return-shipment clock are distinct.

For the map, this means a route can end while the project’s transport system remains active.

## VI. The route is better understood as a braid of counterflows

A simple “person route + object route” is useful at the UI level, but the historical system rapidly becomes a braid of independently timed packets.

Current directionally controlled sequence:

- **westbound:** finished glass U.B. 346–350;
- **westbound:** Rudolf as passenger;
- **westbound:** Hosterwitz repair pigments/materials posted to Cambridge;
- **eastbound:** Cambridge Botanic Garden seeds/instructions to Hosterwitz;
- **southbound/outward and northbound/return:** Rudolf + Cameron + field apparatus through Jamaica, then reference packets back toward Cambridge;
- **eastbound:** Jamaican seeds and later preserved/herbarium/reference materials toward Hosterwitz;
- **bidirectional / asynchronous:** letters and telegrams governing route decisions and supplies;
- **eastbound after person return:** preserved journey specimens still catching up with the workshop;
- **eastbound:** purchased/forwarded books and reference literature;
- **westbound later:** selected American plant studies re-emerge as finished glass models for Harvard.

The central pattern is therefore not “objects circulate while a man travels.” It is **a braided transport system in which the workshop exists across differently timed packets and repeated reassembly**.

This should become the map’s eventual data ontology. It should not become an undifferentiated spaghetti diagram. Packets should be switchable, with individual source guards and latency visible.

## VII. Historiographical calibration: what is and is not new

### 20. “Knowledge circulates” is already an established HPS claim

James Secord’s “Knowledge in Transit” made communication/circulation central to the history of science. Kapil Raj’s *Relocating Modern Science* made circulation and encounter constitutive of knowledge production rather than a secondary diffusion stage. *Mobile Museums* explicitly foregrounds the movement and re-mobilisation of museum collections. None of these literatures leaves conceptual room for claiming novelty merely by showing movement.

### 21. “Logistics and transport matter to natural history” is also established

John McAleer’s study of William Henry Harvey explicitly addresses collecting, preservation, safe transport, specialist luggage, local agents, customs problems and the material constraints of travel. His conclusion enumerates the linked activities required for natural-history collecting, including carrying, transporting, transferring, shipping and preserving. Recent work by Reese, Finney and Ville on Australian museum zoological exchange likewise situates natural-history mobility within post-1870 improvements in global transport, communications and payment systems and treats shipping/logistics as staff- and resource-intensive.

Therefore an article whose argument is “Blaschka science depended on logistics” would arrive late to an existing conversation.

### 22. The stronger contribution is mechanism-level

The Blaschka case can move beyond these literatures because the source base lets us reconstruct several things simultaneously at unusually fine grain:

1. **decomposition** — one production project is divided into maker, finished glass, repair toolkit, seeds, preserved specimens, drawings, books, letters, telegrams and documents;
2. **heterogeneous regimes** — freight, passenger travel, UPU post, bonded customs movement, museum customs inspection, botanical-garden supply and local commodity transport are analytically distinguishable;
3. **media selection** — transport constraints affect whether botanical knowledge exists as living plant, seed, drawing, herbarium sheet, alcohol specimen or published reference;
4. **handoff authority** — brokers, customs officers, museum porters, botanists, technicians and makers acquire different temporary powers over objects and packets;
5. **latency** — people, materials, references and decisions demonstrably arrive on different clocks;
6. **reassembly** — Cambridge and Hosterwitz repeatedly reconstruct workable ensembles from packets that did not travel together;
7. **feedback** — later Blaschka evidence shows receiver reports, vibration, quarantine classification and skilled unpacking feeding back into packing and route design.

The novelty should therefore be stated at the level of **how a distributed workshop was made operational**, not at the level of mobility itself.

## VIII. Longitudinal comparison: 1892 is an unusually visible knot in a durable system

The GitHub logistics registers provide later cases that should function as controls rather than evidence back-projected into 1892.

### 23. 1923: regulation reorganizes packing and inspection

The 1923 four-case shipment preserves German export/customs inspection, sealing, Dresden-to-Bremen rail dispatch, Ehrhorn/Emden/Mayer forwarding, New York-to-Boston bonded movement, E. A. Snow brokerage and a U.S. customs/freight invoice. The case shows that customs can force the workshop to redesign the encounter with the state, not merely pay a fee.

### 24. 1929: protective material becomes regulated material

The 1929 sequence makes the point with exceptional clarity. Straw/hay functions as shock protection but triggers U.S. plant/quarantine concerns. The same material can therefore be technically beneficial and administratively hazardous. This is a later comparator for the general proposition that transport changes the legal/epistemic state of materials.

### 25. 1930–32: packing is a temporary object-state followed by museum remounting

The 1930 procedural source describes cardboard supports, inner boxes, tissue immobilisation, pine outer cases, straw/burlap shock protection, customs opening, removal of temporary supports and final plaster remounting. The 1932 correspondence then explicitly credits Bierweiler’s skilled unpacking for an exceptionally successful arrival.

These later cases should be used to demonstrate that the 1892 distributed-handling pattern became a durable and increasingly explicit packing–unpacking system. Their exact materials/methods must not be projected backward onto the 1892 U.B. 346–350 crates without direct evidence.

## IX. Source-critical corrections and confidence rules after the full pass

### A. Split the existing 1892 consignment cluster

The older `CONS-1892-01` event should be conceptually decomposed into at least:

- January–February U.B. 346–350 freight movement and receiving chain;
- Rudolf’s separate outbound passenger route;
- Hosterwitz → Cambridge repair-material post;
- June Cambridge repair of accumulated older damage;
- separate seed/reference packets.

### B. Do not attach June severe customs damage to U.B. 346–350

The “first consignment” language and Rossi-Wilcox chronology point backward to the initial botanical shipment; the 1892 shipment has separate sampled good-condition evidence.

### C. Downgrade/repair `PACK-1892-CUSTOMS-OPENING` before public reuse

`L.B. 1–5` and the whole-shipment “without even a scratch” wording are not supported by the direct sequence isolated in this audit. Preserve the current file for provenance until a correction commit is made with a short source note.

### D. Matching sailing ≠ direct bill of lading

*Elbe*, 30 January 1892, is a strong contextual match from the Bremen departure database. Keep the vessel/date at `corroborated_context` until cargo paperwork is found.

### E. UPU category link is a strong inference, not yet packet-level proof

The 1878/1885 postal rules and Rudolf’s “sample without value” wording align closely. Confirm formal use through wrapper/postage/customs evidence if available.

### F. Sampled condition remains sampled

One examined *Datura* and roughly eight inspected specimens in excellent condition cannot be generalized to every model in all unopened cases.

### G. Taxon continuity remains many-to-many

The Rakow/Corning finding aids often date drawings “1892 or 1895.” A taxon’s presence in drawing, herbarium and later model records is not sufficient by itself to claim a unique 1892 field-reference → model genealogy.

## XI. Map implications, held for the next implementation pass

The pass-10 page architecture remains sound: full-width map; Journey / Work / Flows as analytical mode; Person / Objects / Both as route scope; evidence strip below; packet directory.

The research now suggests a stricter object-route grammar for the next implementation pass:

- each packet gets a stable packet ID;
- each leg has `origin`, `destination`, `medium`, `carrier/infrastructure`, `regime`, `handler`, `state_before`, `state_after`, `evidence_status` and `latency` where known;
- legal/regulatory state changes are visible separately from geographic motion;
- source-guarded intermediate anchors are visually distinguished from exact documented nodes;
- packets can be selected individually so the map never requires showing the full braid at once;
- `Both` should make temporal divergence legible rather than simply overlay more lines;
- the next two packets should probably be the **repair-material post** and **February Cambridge seeds**, because both have explicit origin/destination, documentary language and transport-specific evidence. The broader Jamaica preserved-specimen packet should wait until its shipment/arrival chronology is better bounded.

No public implementation was made from these recommendations during pass 11.

## XII. Current article-level proposition

The strongest article-level formulation after this audit is:

> **The Blaschka workshop did not travel as a unit. It was repeatedly decomposed into people, finished models, repair materials, botanical references, instructions and documents that moved through different infrastructures and legal categories. Those handoffs changed what the work physically was, who could act on it, and when its parts could be reassembled. The glass model was therefore produced through transport as well as carried by it.**

A more HPS-explicit version:

> **Transportability was an epistemic operation. Living plants, fragile models and workshop techniques could not move in the same form, so the Blaschka commission converted them into differently mobile media—seed, drawing, herbarium or preserved specimen, numbered freight case, postal sample, book, telegram—and coordinated their later reassembly.**

These claims should be tested against object-level examples rather than allowed to become free-standing theory language.

## XIII. Historiographical boundary condition

Do not pitch the project as discovering circulation, materiality, museum mobility, natural-history logistics, or customs problems. Secord, Raj, *Mobile Museums*, McAleer and recent natural-history trade scholarship already make those fields explicit.

The defensible contribution is the unusually reconstructable **mechanism** joining:

- media conversion for transportability;
- temporary handoff authority;
- heterogeneous regulatory regimes;
- asynchronous packet clocks;
- repair and unpacking as distributed skills;
- reassembly into a working workshop or museum object.

1892 is especially valuable because finished glass, a travelling maker, repair materials, living/dried botanical references, instructions and public narratives are all simultaneously visible in a narrow documentary window.
