## Main result

The strongest research object is not simply “Rudolf Blaschka’s American journey.” The archive supports a much more precise reconstruction of **a mobile extension of the Blaschka workshop**.

The journey redistributed model-making across places, people and media. Rudolf did not travel only to collect specimens. He observed living plants, worked with a microscope, made colour studies, preserved material in alcohol, relied on dried herbarium specimens, sent seeds/bulbs/cacti, arranged later supply when flowering windows had closed, and used local botanists and botanical gardens as temporary production infrastructure. Cambridge remained active at a distance through Goodale’s introductions, postal forwarding and telegraphic route decisions. The Dresden workshop also remained active as the destination for living material and the eventual site where these distributed references would be translated into glass.

This gives the project a strong first-order mechanism:

**living organism → field encounter → selective recording/preservation → mobile reference packet → Dresden reassembly → glass model**

The arrows are not automatic. Each transition can fail, be postponed, or be substituted by another reference form.

## Route: planned versus documented

The April planning letter lays out a western route from Cambridge through Chicago, Missouri, Kansas, Colorado, New Mexico and Arizona to San Diego/Los Angeles, with a proposed return through San Francisco and Colorado. The dated correspondence then supplies a dense observed sequence: Cambridge (12 Apr), Chicago (14 Apr), Tempe (20 Apr), Maricopa (24 Apr), San Diego (late Apr–4 May), San Bernardino/Banning/Agua Caliente (4–9 May), Oakland (15 May), Elko (20 May), Colorado Springs (22–29 May), St. Louis (31 May–2 Jun), Chicago (5 Jun), Niagara/Buffalo/Clifton (6 Jun), Cambridge (early–mid June), and New York (17 Jun), with booked departure on the *Aller* on 18 June.

The Jamaica segment is similarly reconstructable from the February departure material and March correspondence. A 6 March Hope Garden letter looks back to the northern-coast movement through Port Antonio, an intervening banana-delivery port, Port Maria and Annotto Bay, followed by overland movement through Castleton to Hope Garden. This is not background colour: the letter describes luggage constraints, glass jars, hired transport, work rooms and Cameron’s role in preparing/drying material.

A core editorial rule follows: **map a planned route and an observed route separately**. The newspapers make this especially necessary.

## Newspaper layer

The new CSV materially improves the research in two ways.

First, it reveals a syndication family. The *American Register* of 16 April 1892 reports Rudolf in Jamaica studying plants and says he will soon go to Cambridge. U.S. papers in May and June repeat a formula in which he will visit Cambridge and then travel west collecting “typical American flowers” before returning to Germany and reproducing them in glass. These are valuable contemporary sources, but their publication dates often post-date stages Rudolf had already completed. They therefore preserve the **publicly circulating plan**, not a real-time itinerary.

Second, retrospective newspaper material makes the epistemic chain explicit. A 1894 *New-York Tribune* item recalls the western trip, including California and Arizona, and connects colour studies / collected material to later model production. This is useful corroboration for purpose and reception, while exact quantities still require image-level checking.

This means newspaper chronology can itself become evidence: compare the date of a reprinted route statement with the dated letters to measure informational lag and the persistence of a projected journey in public print.

## The distributed workshop

Several episodes now look especially strong.

**Jamaica.** Cameron is not merely a guide. Rudolf says Cameron provides plant material and prepares specimens in white rum/water and for herbaria. Hope and Castleton provide rooms, local expertise and access. The Jamaican Botanical Department later reports 116 accurate coloured drawings and corresponding dried collections. A cited 27 March Rudolf letter gives 106 completed species. The 106/116 discrepancy should be preserved until the chronology and counting categories are checked.

**San Diego.** Local botanists, especially Orcutt, expand what Rudolf can record. A translated 4 May letter says he completed 36 species there, collected cacti, and sent a small box of cacti and flowering bulbs to Cambridge. A later Cambridge letter reports receiving a *Crinum* bulb and seeds from Orcutt after the party had left California, with Rudolf planning an arrangement for future seeds and bulbs when flowering seasons had been missed. This is one of the clearest demonstrations that the journey did not end when Rudolf left a locality.

**California route choice.** The Shasta/Darlingtonia episode should remain unresolved for now. One translated passage says the party waited for Goodale’s telegraphic decision and received an instruction not to go to Shasta; another later translation can be read as though they had travelled there for *Darlingtonia*. This is precisely the kind of conflict that should not be flattened. Check the German originals and page images before drawing the route through Shasta.

**St. Louis.** A local contact connected through Ganong arranges accommodation, and the botanical garden provides a work room. Rudolf describes making a colour sketch and preserving a specimen in alcohol. A botanical institution is functioning as a temporary workshop station, not simply as a place visited.

**Cambridge on return.** The trip closes without a clean separation between fieldwork and manufacture. Rudolf repairs damaged Harvard models while still receiving Californian plant material and organizing his return voyage. Collection, repair, communication and future production overlap.

## Archive objects that can tighten the route

The 05B finding-aid layer contains a useful ephemera cluster: 1892 travel-insurance receipts, the *Saale* passenger list, a Southern California Railway timetable, a San Diego County map, a Santa Fé / Pacific Coast guide covering California–Arizona–New Mexico–Colorado–Kansas, Niagara guides, and photographic/souvenir albums of Jamaica, San Francisco and the American Southwest. These should be crosswalked against letter dates rather than treated as decorative travel memorabilia.

The photographic series is also important because the finding aid explicitly identifies William F. Ganong as Rudolf’s 1892 assistant and includes “Fieldwork in the American Southwest, circa 1892” and photographs of Ganong with Rudolf. This provides a visual/material witness distinct from the letters.

## Research direction after this pass

The project can now move from “mapping a trip” to **reconstructing how a scientific-artisanal workshop operated at continental scale before the object was made**. The most productive units are not cities but transformations and dependencies: bloom/no bloom; living/dried/alcohol; drawing/specimen/seed; local collector/remote patron; train/steamship/post/telegraph; planned route/actual route; field reference/later glass.

The paid newspaper corpus should be appended as a delta. Its highest value will be (1) finding local reports that can pin down stops or named contacts, (2) identifying the syndication ancestor of the April–June route story, and (3) locating reception language about what Rudolf was understood to be collecting or learning. Newspaper hit counts should not be used quantitatively until syndication duplicates are clustered.
