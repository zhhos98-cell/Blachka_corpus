# Rudolf 1892 yield governance / bilateral selection pass 26 — 13 August 2026

## Purpose

Pass 25 showed that failed access could be repaired by substitution, later supply, live carriage, remote decision or rejection. This raises a larger counting problem. The 1892 archive is full of numbers: 25 useful species at Tempe, 200 drawn and studied by Colorado Springs, a possible 400 by departure, a counterfactual 600–800 for a year-long trip, about three years of material in Rudolf's June estimate, and about four years in Goodale's post-departure estimate.

Those numbers should not be reconciled into one expedition total. They count different operations.

The strongest formulation after this pass is:

> **The expedition's yield was governed, not simply harvested. Field selection, Harvard's existing collection, season, travel duration, Rudolf's work rate, identification infrastructure and future workshop capacity all intervened between plants encountered and models that could eventually be made.**

This also makes selection genuinely distributed. Rudolf selected in the field, Cambridge filtered against existing coverage, patrons and Goodale negotiated feasible output, and the workshop converted the surviving reference stock into future labour.

No public map changes are made.

---

## 1. Before the journey: model output is made explicitly elastic

The 15 February Cambridge letter (`BLA-D00682 / 017:36`) records a decision made by Rudolf with Mrs. and Miss Ware and accepted by Goodale.

They will **not hold themselves to exactly sixty species**. Instead, they will make as many as can be **comfortably** produced, with fifty as the minimum.

This is important because quantity is already tied to work capacity before the field expedition starts.

The commission is therefore not:

`collect 60 species → make 60 models`.

It is closer to:

`minimum expectation + flexible upper output constrained by what can be made well`.

The model-production target is elastic from the outset.

---

## 2. Tempe: encountered flora is reduced by representational selection

Pass 21 established the 22 April Tempe rule (`BLA-D00785 / 017:139`). Most very small desert plants are ignored. The party concentrates on the showiest and most characteristic forms and arrives at twenty-five species useful to them.

The `25` therefore does not mean twenty-five species existed, or even twenty-five species were encountered.

It is a number produced after selection:

`flora encountered → evaluated → useful subset = 25`.

This is the first quantitative gate.

---

## 3. Colorado Springs: Cambridge filters the field from a distance

The Colorado Springs letter (`BLA-D00879 / 017:233`) is the clearest evidence that yield is also governed from the receiving side.

Rudolf says that this time of year there is not a large selection and that the people in Cambridge are `picky`: they do not want some species because those species are already represented.

So a plant can pass the field-side test and still fail the institutional one.

`botanically available ≠ useful addition to Harvard`.

The expedition's useful reference corpus is therefore partly defined by the pre-existing museum collection.

This is a very different mechanism from field scarcity. A species can be in flower and easily accessible but institutionally redundant.

---

## 4. The same letter links yield to Rudolf's body and travel duration

Rudolf also says he is working in a casual/moderate manner so as not to overtax himself. He rejects the idea that simply working faster would make the total infinitely expandable.

He says that to reach **600–800 species** he would have to wander around America for the whole year, which he does not want to do.

At that moment he reports **200 species drawn and studied** and thinks he might have **400** by departure.

These quantities belong to different categories:

- 200 = current drawn/studied throughput;
- 400 = projected departure yield;
- 600–800 = counterfactual yield requiring a radically longer itinerary.

The source itself ties the difference to season, available time and bodily work.

So throughput is not simply a function of botanical abundance.

---

## 5. Botanical gardens alter the cost of producing a usable reference

In the same Colorado letter, Rudolf explains why he expects St Louis to be productive. In a good botanical garden, material is easier to find because **the names and everything are at hand**.

This is a small but important point.

A botanical garden can increase usable-reference yield without increasing raw species abundance. It compresses identification and search work.

The relevant resource is therefore not only the plant. It is:

`plant + name + organized collection + knowledgeable staff + accessible workspace`.

This helps explain why St Louis can substitute for a more expensive habitat expedition in the Darlingtonia case.

---

## 6. June: field counts are converted into workshop time

At Cambridge in June (`BLA-D00927 / 017:282`), Rudolf says that through drawing and studying what had been collected he had acquired about **three years of material**. He emphasizes the limited number of working days and says he tried not to neglect anything in flower, even small weeds.

This is a new unit.

The trip's output is no longer expressed as a species count. It becomes anticipated duration of future work.

Goodale then produces an independent estimate after Rudolf's departure (`BLA-D00091 / 015:91`): in his view Rudolf has material on hand for about **four years' work**.

These are actor-specific valuations and should remain separate.

There is no reason to average them into 3.5 years. The difference is historically useful because it shows that `work horizon` was an estimate generated by different people for different decisions.

Goodale's estimate occurs in a discussion of remuneration, the contract and Leopold's future active work. It is therefore partly managerial.

---

## 7. A yield ontology is now necessary

The current archive should stop using `species collected` as a default container for every number.

At minimum, quantitative records need separate fields for:

- encountered or considered;
- selected as useful;
- drawn;
- microscopically studied;
- colour-recorded;
- alcohol-preserved;
- herbarium-dried;
- seeds/bulbs/live plants acquired;
- sent or forwarded;
- rejected as already represented;
- cultivated later;
- modelled later;
- model sets produced;
- estimated years of future work.

The same plant can occupy several of these categories, while a count at one stage cannot be substituted for a count at another.

This also gives us a principled way to retain the Jamaica 106/116 problem and other apparent numerical contradictions. A discrepancy may expose different counting operations rather than poor arithmetic.

---

## 8. Selection is bilateral, then multilateral

The current workflow can now be represented as several gates.

### Field gate

Rudolf and collaborators choose what is useful, characteristic, showy, safe and feasible to record.

### Museum gate

Cambridge asks whether the reference adds something to the existing collection or simply duplicates it.

### Itinerary/environment gate

Season and travel time determine which plant states are available during the visit.

### Body/work gate

Rudolf's ability to draw, study and preserve material limits daily throughput.

### Workshop gate

The final reference stock is translated into a feasible programme of future glass work.

The field collector is therefore not sovereign over yield. Selection is distributed across people, institutions, environments and later work capacity.

---

## 9. Paper-level consequence

A raw number of species makes the 1892 expedition look like a collecting success that can be compared to another expedition by size.

The archive supports a more interesting question:

> **How was a potential botanical abundance converted into an institutionally useful and technically feasible production queue?**

That conversion can now be followed through explicit historical decisions:

`60 loosened to ≥50 comfortable output → field filtering → Cambridge duplicate rejection → season/time/body constraints → stabilized references → three/four-year work-horizon estimates → later selective model production`.

This gives the numbers causal meaning.

---

## 10. Map / figure decision

No new route layer.

The useful visualization is a **filter/conversion diagram**:

`encountered flora`
→ `field-selected`
→ `Harvard nonduplicate`
→ `stabilized reference`
→ `workshop queue`
→ `finished model`.

Numbers should appear only beside the stage they actually count.

This would make the map/evidence interface more source-critical and prevent a single `species total` from swallowing distinct operations.
