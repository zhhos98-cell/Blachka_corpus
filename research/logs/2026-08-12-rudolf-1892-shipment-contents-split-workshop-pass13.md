# Rudolf 1892 shipment contents + split-workshop pass 13 — 12 August 2026

## Purpose

Continue the transport/handoff investigation after passes 11–12 by connecting four evidence layers that had previously remained only partly joined: the 1892 travel correspondence, Harvard's annotated shipment list, later shipment correspondence in 1893–1894, and Rudolf's retrospective authorship explanation of 1900. The public map is deliberately left unchanged in this pass. The aim is to test whether the transport argument survives at model-number and production-sequence level.

It does, but the mechanism needs a further refinement. The workshop did not merely decompose into mobile packets. In 1892 it was also **split between a travelling maker and a stationary maker**, while completed models, repair materials, plant references, books, instructions and later model consignments moved on different clocks. The expedition was not reassembled once on Rudolf's return. It was metabolized into production over several later shipments.

The strongest revised formulation is:

> **1892 split the Blaschka workshop without stopping production. One maker travelled, one continued manufacturing at Hosterwitz, and heterogeneous reference and model packets moved between America, Harvard and the workshop on different clocks. Later shipments serially reassembled work produced at different times, places and under different evidential genealogies.**

A second result follows: **a shipment is an aggregation technology, not a production-time unit**. A single consignment can flatten together objects made months apart, work produced by different makers, field-derived models, cryptogamic/anatomical models and older inventory.

## 1. Correction: the January 1892 sixth shipment is model nos. 252–311

The Harvard annotated shipment list provides a tighter object crosswalk for U.B.346–350 than the map previously had.

The relevant OCR sequence reads:

- `251. Trillium grandiflorum ...`
- then the heading **`6th. Shipment January, 1892.`**
- then `252. Actaea alba ...`
- the sequence continues through `311. Agrimonia ...`
- then the heading **`7th. Shipment, Feb. 1893.`**

Thus the sixth shipment's numbered model range is **252–311 inclusive**, exactly 60 model numbers. This matches the 17 December 1891 workshop letter saying a series of 60 plant species was nearing completion and the 20 January 1892 dispatch letter stating that U.B.346–350 contained 60 plant models plus 329 analytical details.

This corrects a tempting but wrong range of 251–310. Model 251 sits before the sixth-shipment heading and must not be silently absorbed into it. No evidence yet allocates individual model numbers among cases U.B.346, 347, 348, 349 and 350, so the public object route should continue to treat the five cases as one shipment packet until a case-level packing/content list is found.

Evidence:
- Harvard `Annotated shipment list of models`, Series V, Subseries I, OCR Part 1 / Part 3 in 05B.
- BLA-D00006, 17 Dec 1891: 60 species nearing completion.
- BLA-D00016 / 015:16, 20 Jan 1892: U.B.346–350, 60 models, 329 analytical details.

Evidence status: direct primary crosswalk; model-to-case allocation unresolved.

## 2. Datura model 290: the object can move before the field encounter

The shipment list makes the February Harvard unpacking much more specific. **Model 290 is Datura arborea** and belongs to the January 1892 sixth shipment. A cleaner OCR of the list gives five analytical details for model 290. In the 19 February Cambridge letter, Rudolf says that when Goodale opened one of the newly arrived boxes, they took out a Datura arborea and found it well preserved. Thus model 290 can be followed from numbered shipment list to the Harvard unpacking scene.

Weeks later, during the Jamaica journey, Rudolf describes a veranda surrounded by the wonderful blossoms of `Datura arbora`. This is a field encounter with the living counterpart after the Harvard representation had already been made, packed, carried across the Atlantic and unpacked. The episode is therefore a useful control on any linear model of the 1892 trip as `field encounter → reference → glass`. At least for Datura 290, the order visible in 1892 is the reverse: **finished representation → transport/unpacking → later encounter with a living counterpart**.

This does **not** prove that the Jamaica encounter changed, corrected or validated model 290. No such causal statement is in the source. Its value is chronological: a field journey can add experience to a species already represented in glass.

Rudolf's 7 August 1900 letter adds an authorship layer. He explains that joint authorship was highly amalgamated and often impossible to divide cleanly. He nevertheless lists models in which Leopold's hand predominated in the glass shaping; nos. **290–291 Datura** appear in that list. In the same letter Rudolf says that every model in the Harvard collection passed through his hands for painting and finishing, and that during the joint period painting had become his regular share. The strongest guarded attribution for model 290 is therefore: **Leopold's hand predominated in the glasswork; Rudolf states that he painted/finished the joint-period models.** It would be inaccurate to call 290 a model made solely by Leopold.

Technical conservation independently anchors the object/date identity. McNally and Buschini's 1993 materials study analyzed original adhesive from **Datura model 290 (1892)** and identified it as animal/hide glue; the related paint-media section identifies hide-glue binder on Datura model 291 and a probable natural-resin binder on model 318. These technical results are object evidence, not maker-attribution evidence.

Evidence:
- Harvard annotated shipment list: model 290 Datura arborea, sixth shipment.
- BLA-D00695 / 017:49: Datura unpacked at Harvard, 19 Feb 1892.
- BLA-D00711–12: Jamaica Datura blossoms, March 1892 letter sequence.
- BLA-D01746–49: Rudolf to Mary Ware, 7 Aug 1900, authorship/technique discussion.
- JAIC 32.3 (1993), McNally & Buschini, technical analysis of models 290/291/318.

Guard: chronological relation only; no claim of correction/calibration after Jamaica.

## 3. The stationary workshop: Leopold manufactured while Rudolf travelled

The annotated shipment list contains a remarkably explicit note at the start of the seventh shipment:

**`(#312-322 made by Leopold Blaschka, in the first half year 1892.)`**

The eleven models are:
312 Clintonia borealis; 313 Saxifraga umbrosa; 314 Saxifraga trifurcata (OCR spelling varies); 315 Aquilegia canadensis; 316 Podophyllum peltatum; 317 Rhododendron praecox; 318 Phyllocactus ackermannii; 319 Phyllocactus phyllanthoides; 320 Polypodium aureum; 321 Claviceps purpurea; 322 Sedum pulchellum.

Rudolf's 1900 retrospective letter independently recalls **“the 11 models my father has prepared from January till July 1892”** and says the list had been sent to Goodale in July 1892. The same 1900 letter quotes an earlier letter of 30 March 1892 in which Leopold reportedly said that he was working on the plants as far as possible and they could be finished when his son returned. This last quotation is retrospective and the underlying 30 March letter has not yet been isolated in the current corpus; it therefore remains a quoted retrospective witness rather than a direct 1892 document.

The travelling correspondence also catches the other side of this split. In Colorado, Rudolf answers his father's report about work at home: `Dear father you write to me about your work. Take it easy...` The continuation is OCR-fragmentary, but the exchange independently shows workshop work as an active topic while Rudolf is in the western United States.

This materially changes the “mobile workshop” thesis. During the journey, Hosterwitz did not wait for the traveller to return. **Leopold continued glass production while Rudolf accumulated field reference and later supplied finishing work.** The workshop was simultaneously stationary and mobile.

Most importantly, the eleven models made in the first half of 1892 did not become a shipment in the first half of 1892. They appear under the **seventh shipment, February 1893**. Their production clock and shipment clock are separated by many months. This gives direct evidence for an inventory/queue phase between manufacture and consignment.

## 4. Seventh shipment, February 1893: one consignment aggregates different histories

The annotated list begins the seventh shipment at model 312 and the next, eighth shipment begins at 374. The seventh shipment therefore spans **312–373** in the annotated sequence.

This one consignment already contains heterogeneous temporal and epistemic layers:

1. models 312–322, explicitly made by Leopold in the first half of 1892;
2. a substantial cryptogamic/anatomical block beginning with Claviceps, Arcyria, Physarum, Fucus, Marchantia, Funaria, Atrichum, Lycopodium and structural models;
3. later ordinary plant models in the same shipment.

Goodale's letter of 31 January 1893 to the Wares says that the coming material includes **“a few of the American plants collected on the journey, and a large group of Cryptogamia.”** The annotated February shipment independently shows the large cryptogamic component. The phrase “a few of the American plants” proves that some journey material had begun to return as glass by this shipment, although the current pass does not assign individual model numbers to that phrase without further label/model crosswalk.

The point is structural: **shipment seven is not a coherent production episode.** It combines stationary work made during Rudolf's absence with later scientific/cryptogamic work and at least some journey-derived American plants. Freight makes these heterogeneous histories appear as one consignment.

This is the first strong example of `shipment as aggregation technology`.

## 5. Eighth shipment, July/August 1893: the expedition becomes a quantified production block

The 18 July 1893 Hosterwitz letter is the strongest bridge from fieldwork to later shipment. It says the present consignment is nearly ready and will be dispatched at the end of July or in the first days of August. It then divides the first part into **37 species of plants and their details in the usual style**:

- **20** made after studies in Jamaica;
- **15** from the West;
- OCR reads `E` for the final count, but 37 − 20 − 15 = **2**, and the sentence says these were after garden material. The value 2 is therefore an explicit arithmetic reconstruction, not a silent OCR correction.

The next page names several anchors: Passiflora murucuja and Kingston's `Kill-buckra-weed` identified as Tribulus among the Jamaica material; Viola, Rosa gymnocarpa and Rosa woodsii among western plants. The letter also says the second portion of the same consignment comprised higher-science/anatomy/physiology models, especially Cryptogams.

The annotated shipment list is unusually compatible with this breakdown. The heading **`8th Shipment, July 1893`** precedes model 374; models **374–410 inclusive are exactly 37 numbered plant models**. Models 411–423 then form a scientific/cryptogamic/anatomical block before the ninth-shipment heading. Within 374–410:

- 374–393 = 20 model numbers;
- 394–408 = 15;
- 409–410 = 2.

The sequence also contains named anchors from the letter: Tribulus at 384; Passiflora murucuja at 391; Rosa woodsii at 396; Rosa gymnocarpa at 397; Viola at 398. This makes the partition **374–393 Jamaica / 394–408 West / 409–410 garden material** a strong sequence-based reconstruction.

It is still a reconstruction. The OCR/list does not itself print regional headings over those number ranges, so the boundary assignment should remain labelled `high-confidence inferred block`, pending inspection of the original list image or model labels. The exact numerical fit plus the named anchor species make it much stronger than a generic association.

Models 411–423 then show why a consignment is not a single epistemic category: Puccinia, Urocystis, Tilletia, Tetraphis, Salvinia, Marsilia, Selaginella and anther-development models travel in the same shipment as Jamaica and western field-derived flowering plants.

This is serial reassembly in its clearest form. The 1892 expedition is converted into a 1893 outbound model block, but it shares the crate/shipment horizon with scientific work generated through other reference systems.

## 6. Ninth shipment, January 1894: the journey's production afterlife continues

The 15 December 1893 workshop letter says the January consignment will contain **50 species, exclusively flowers and their details**, made with only a few stated exceptions from the material and studies of Jamaica and the West. It names economic and western material, including Anacardium, Coca, Eucalyptus and Darlingtonia californica, and stresses that many of these had never been available in bloom in European gardens.

The annotated list places the heading **`9th Shipment, January, 1894`** before model 424 and the **`10th Shipment, August, 1894`** heading after model 474, before model 475. A naive inclusive count of 424–474 yields 51 numbers, while the December planning letter says 50 species. That discrepancy is retained open. It could reflect list structure, an additional/non-species entry, a shipment change after the planning letter, or an OCR/layout issue. The current pass does not force a range of 424–473 merely because it gives the desired arithmetic.

The more important result is independent of that one-number problem: **well over a year after Rudolf returned, the workshop still described an entire major consignment as overwhelmingly dependent on Jamaica and western American material.**

A 3 November 1894 letter extends the afterlife further. Leopold and Rudolf say that some American material takes longer than expected; that it gives them great pleasure to prepare models of plants collected in America; that local botanists and gardeners were seeing Jamaica and West-American flowers they had never seen blooming; and that for many Arizona and California plants sketched on the journey no picture existed in books. They already had further models in preparation for the next shipment.

This gives substantial retrospective support to Rudolf's June 1892 estimate that his drawing, study and collecting had accumulated roughly **“three years of material.”** The phrase was a contemporary estimate, not a literal production ledger, but the 1893–94 shipment sequence shows that the expedition's reference corpus remained productive for years rather than one immediate post-return batch.

## 7. Transport medium changes what can later be known or made

The western journey correspondence contains a particularly useful micro-mechanism that should be retained for the transport argument.

At Agua Caliente Rudolf says Ganong handled plants in **glass containers with alcohol and water, and with dried specimens**, and that two boxes of material had already been sent to Cambridge, where they would remain until Rudolf was ready to return home (BLA-D00829). Cambridge therefore functioned as a staging/cache node, not merely a destination.

In the next sequence Rudolf discusses cacti already sent toward his parents and explicitly says it is **better to receive them alive than in alcohol, because “we can grow the flower ourselves”** (BLA-D00840). Three of those cacti had been collected in Jamaica.

This is unusually strong evidence that choice of transport/preservation medium was epistemically consequential. Alcohol could stabilize a specimen's morphology for carriage; living transport preserved a future developmental event, flowering, that the workshop wanted to observe later. A plant was not made “mobile” in one generic sense. Different mobile states preserved different future affordances.

The San Diego sequence adds viability risk and staging. Rudolf completed 36 species and sent a small box of cacti and flowering bulbs, with other things, to Cambridge for Goodale to forward to Hosterwitz; he worried that a giant-cactus cutting might rot. After the party had left California, Orcutt continued the flow by sending a Crinum bulb and a seed packet. Rudolf then planned a larger future-supply arrangement for species whose flowering times the travellers had been unable to wait for.

This produces a stronger mechanism than circulation alone:

`living plant → choose what future property must survive → select medium (alive / alcohol / dried / drawing / seed) → transport or stage → later observation / cultivation / modelling`.

## 8. Revised analytical architecture

The 1892 project now needs at least four concurrent lanes:

1. **Bodily lane** — Rudolf's passenger route and field/repair work.
2. **Finished-object lane** — U.B.346–350, model nos.252–311, moving west as freight/bonded/customs/museum objects.
3. **Reference/material/information lanes** — repair materials, seeds, cacti, bulbs, preserved material, drawings, books, letters and telegrams moving asynchronously in both directions.
4. **Stationary-production lane** — Leopold making models 312–322 at Hosterwitz while Rudolf travelled, with those objects later queued into the February 1893 shipment.

The post-journey history adds a fifth temporal layer: **serial outbound reassembly**. The expedition's references become glass across shipment seven (some American plants), shipment eight (quantified 20 Jamaica + 15 West + 2 garden block), shipment nine (majority Jamaica/West material), and still later work in 1894.

This suggests three claims stronger than generic “knowledge in transit”:

### A. The workshop was split, not simply mobile
One maker's body moved while the other maker and the physical workshop continued production. Distance work depended on coupling those sites through shipments, post, reference media and later finishing.

### B. Shipment is an aggregation technology
A consignment compresses heterogeneous production times and genealogies into a single transport event. Shipment seven is the clearest control: first-half-1892 Leopold production, cryptogamic work and some journey plants enter the same February 1893 shipment.

### C. Transportability is productive
A living cactus and an alcohol specimen are not equivalent carriers of the same information. The medium chosen for movement changes what can later be observed and therefore what the workshop can make.

A useful compact formulation is:

> **The workshop operated by splitting production, packetizing reference, and serially aggregating unlike work into shipments. Transport did not merely carry finished representation; it organized the timing, medium and later recombination of production.**

## 9. Source guards and unresolved problems

1. **Sixth shipment boundary:** 252–311 is strongly established by heading placement and the exact 60-model count. Original list-image inspection remains desirable before publication.
2. **Case allocation:** no current source assigns models 252–311 among U.B.346–350 individually.
3. **Datura 290:** later Jamaica encounter is chronological evidence only; no correction/validation claim.
4. **Authorship:** Rudolf's 1900 explanation is retrospective and explicitly says authorship is amalgamated. `Leopold predominant in glasswork` is stronger than `Leopold made it`.
5. **Leopold 312–322:** annotated list is direct; 1900 letter corroborates retrospectively. Underlying July 1892 list to Goodale and March 30 1892 letter remain retrieval targets.
6. **Seventh shipment American plants:** Goodale says “a few”; individual model assignments remain open.
7. **Eighth shipment regional ranges:** 374–393 / 394–408 / 409–410 are high-confidence sequence inference based on exact 20/15/2 arithmetic plus named species; not yet printed regional headings.
8. **Ninth shipment:** primary letter says 50 species while annotated heading geometry appears to span 424–474. Preserve mismatch.
9. **Harvard staging:** two boxes were intended to stay in Cambridge until Rudolf was ready to return. Harvard's finding aid separately says >300 herbarium specimens from the 1892/1895 expeditions remained in Harvard after his return. Intended staging must not be equated with proven onward shipment; specimen-level routing remains open.
10. **Postal categories:** “sample/example without value” resembles contemporary postal sample vocabulary, but wrappers/receipts are still needed for exact classification.
