# Rudolf 1892 Snow settlement + exhibition transport pass 15 — 13 August 2026

## Purpose

Continue the transport investigation after the split-workshop, shipment-output, commercial-infrastructure and specimen/model passes by asking what happens **after a shipment has physically arrived**.

Two previously underused source clusters answer this with unusual precision:

1. E. A. Snow's receiving-side bills and customs arrangements, 1892–1894, followed longitudinally into 1906 and 1923;
2. the May 1893 Chicago exhibition correspondence, in which models that had survived express carriage were subsequently remobilized and broken by their display environment.

The result adds two clocks to the project:

- **settlement/accounting clock** — bills, omitted charges, broker commission, customs expenses and inspector carriage can remain open after physical arrival;
- **installation/exhibition clock** — unpacked and displayed objects can become mobile again, requiring repair and redesign of their supports.

The strongest revised formulation is:

> **Arrival was not the end of transport. A Blaschka shipment could continue administratively through settlement and customs release, and materially through installation, vibration, breakage, repair and re-stabilization.**

This pass also establishes that an invoice can function as a retrospective route map: Snow's March 1893 bill compresses German rail, ocean carriage, a Sound-steamer leg, customs, inspector transport and broker commission into one receiving-side document.

No public map changes are made in this pass. The aim is to harden the historical architecture and distinguish direct route evidence from tempting route reconstruction.

## 1. E. A. Snow is now a longitudinal infrastructure actor, not a stray customs name

The direct correspondence allows a much longer Snow chronology than the current 1892 map implies.

### February 1892 — papers and inspector

For U.B. 346–350, Goodale receives advice that five cases have arrived by *Elbe* and turns the papers over to **E. A. Snow** (`BLA-D00024 / 015:24`). A few days later Goodale writes that Snow has sent **Capt. Wales of the Custom House** to Harvard, where about eight specimens are opened and found in excellent condition (`BLA-D00028 / 015:28`).

Snow is therefore already situated at the junction between documentary handoff and authorized physical opening. The archive does not need a guessed company biography to establish his function.

### March 1893 — the transport chain appears in invoice form

`BLA-D00140 / 015:140`, dated 28 March 1893, is the strongest newly isolated document in this pass. Goodale says that he has sufficient cash to pay **$106.97** to E. A. Snow for the immediately preceding consignment. The OCR describing the number/type of cases is unsafe and should not be normalized without the manuscript image. The charge categories, however, are substantially clearer.

Goodale says the bill covers:

- all railroad bills in Germany;
- freight by the **Ocean and the Sound Steamers**;
- customs expenses;
- carriage for the inspector;
- Snow's commission.

One additional local-freight phrase is badly corrupted in OCR and is deliberately omitted from the reconstruction.

The chronology strongly links this settlement to the seventh shipment, whose production and dispatch are documented from December 1892 through January/February 1893. But the bill itself is more important than the exact case count: it reconstructs a multimodal chain after the fact.

**Interpretive result:** an invoice is not merely evidence of cost. It is a compact documentary representation of the route. Movements that were geographically and institutionally separated are retrospectively made commensurable as charge lines.

### Important Sound-steamer guard

The phrase `Ocean and the Sound Steamers` is direct evidence for this **1893** settled shipment. It must not be back-projected onto U.B. 346–350 in 1892.

A contemporary external control shows that `Sound steamers` was a recognizable transport/accounting category in precisely this period. The Central Vermont Railroad annual report for 1892–93 and adjacent years separately enumerates receipts and expenditures for `Sound steamers`, distinct from ordinary freight, express and lake-steamer business. This supports reading Goodale/Snow's phrase as a real modal category rather than a loose synonym for steamship freight. It does **not** identify the Blaschka carrier or exact New York–Boston transfer route.

Possible Long Island Sound systems in this period included several New York–New England steamer/rail combinations; none is promoted to the Blaschka route until a Snow bill, waybill, carrier receipt or customs/bond paper names it.

## 2. September 1893: a bill carries memory across shipments

`BLA-D00191–00192 / 015:193–194`, 6 September 1893, makes the settlement clock visible in an even stronger form.

Goodale reports that the cases have reached Cambridge and are safely deposited in the Museum. One case has been opened by the special inspector. Snow then sends a bill covering **all freight and express charges from Hosterwitz to Cambridge**, including the special inspector's expenses.

But Snow also adds the carriage from **Col. Wales's previous visit**, explaining that this charge had been omitted from the former bill. Goodale sends the account onward to the Wares as memoranda `for preservation`.

The same letter supplies Snow's contemporary address:

**E. A. Snow, 42 Summer Street, Boston.**

This gives three results.

First, physical arrival does not close the event. A residual cost can remain unsettled and enter a later account.

Second, accounts have **inter-shipment memory**. One consignment can carry a financial remainder from an earlier customs encounter.

Third, the receivers themselves treat these transport bills as records worth preserving. Accounting documents were part of the collection's administrative memory, not disposable receipts after delivery.

The exact earlier Wales visit responsible for the residual carriage charge remains open. It should not automatically be equated with the February 1892 inspection, because the March 1893 Snow bill itself already includes inspector carriage.

---

## 3. March 1894: importation, inspection and free opening remain separate operations

`BLA-D00239 / 015:242`, 19 March 1894, continues the same pattern. Goodale has received Snow's bill for **commission and expenses on the importation** and regards it as correct. Separately, an inspector has come and `passed` the cases, after which Harvard can open them at leisure.

The dollar amount in OCR is unsafe and is not retained.

The secure sequence is enough:

`physical presence → broker/importation account → official inspection/pass → unrestricted museum opening`.

The object can therefore be in Cambridge without yet being freely available as a museum object.

## 4. Snow becomes procedural memory — and then a failure point

Later correspondence makes the earlier 1892–94 role much more intelligible.

### 1906

`BLA-D02095 / 021:112` records Goodale writing to Harvard's Treasurer because incoming Blaschka cases have again been shipped by Ehrhorn, Emden & Mayer and consigned to E. A. Snow. Goodale identifies Snow as **formerly a Custom-house Broker on State St.**, says he is no longer in business, and adds: **`He always did our work well.`** Harvard now has to hunt Snow up and reconstruct the clearance arrangement. The goods are again intended for the University Museum, to be opened under a custom-house inspector.

This is unusually strong evidence for institutional routine. Snow's name/address had become embedded in the route because it encoded remembered receiving practice.

### 1923

The same memory persists even more dramatically. Rudolf's September 1923 instruction sends R.B. 1–4 Bremen → New York → **in bond to Boston**, care of **E. A. Snow, Customhouse broker and forwarding agent**, for Harvard (`BLA-D02693 / 040:49`).

By November, the Bremen agents are anxious because the Boston agent E. A. Snow `does no more exist`; they ask the American Line to find another agent, and Rudolf thinks this is probably the cause of the delay (`BLA-D00341 / 015:344`).

This is a direct path-dependence experiment. A route can fail even though the ports, railroad and ships still exist, because a remembered intermediary embedded in the documentary chain has disappeared.

The safest historical formulation is therefore:

> **A durable route was partly a durable memory of whom to address, who handled the papers, who summoned the inspector, and who settled the charges.**

## 5. E. A. Snow's identity: what is secure and what remains open

Secure direct primary evidence now establishes:

- 1893: address **42 Summer Street, Boston**;
- 1906: Goodale calls him formerly a **Custom-house Broker on State St.**;
- 1923 shipping instruction: **Customhouse broker and forwarding agent, Boston, Mass.**

Generic public-web searches in this pass did not securely resolve his first name or a separate corporate identity. No plausible directory hit is promoted simply because the initials match.

The correct next source environments are Boston city/business directories, customs-broker licensing/administrative records and NARA Boston RG 36. The National Archives describes its Boston Customs holdings as covering 1839–1966 and including imports/exports, manifests, entries, correspondence, accounting and administrative records. This is an unusually good jurisdiction/record-type match, but no individual Snow/Blaschka federal file is claimed until one is located.

## 6. Chicago 1893: display itself becomes a transport regime

A second source cluster adds a different kind of post-arrival movement.

### Express carriage looks successful

`BLA-D00145 / 015:145` reports Ganong setting up the Harvard models in Chicago for the World's Columbian Exposition. The OCR is poor, but the secure paraphrase is that despite rough treatment by the express company, only two relatively unimportant/easily repaired breaks were initially observed.

This should be image-checked before direct quotation.

### The protective support reverses function

Goodale's 28 May Chicago letter (`BLA-D00149–00150 / 015:149–150`) then describes a different failure. The velvet intended to keep the specimens safe had acted, in the OCR's unusually clear phrase, as **`a machine for moving them in all directions`**. Specimens became huddled and many were broken as a result of movement/vibration in the exhibition setting.

The precise OCR wording naming the source of the vibration is corrupt and is not silently repaired. But the operational sequence is clear enough for source-critical use.

Goodale then creates a work area, repairs fractures until many are scarcely visible, remounts and relabels the models, recovers the case and experimentally tests the revised arrangement by shaking/fanning it before reopening the display.

This is important because it reverses the usual endpoint assumption:

- freight can succeed;
- installation can fail;
- a material chosen to immobilize objects can become an agent of movement;
- receiving/exhibition work then has to redesign stability.

The model is therefore still in a mobility regime after it has become an exhibit.

Harvard's modern history confirms that the permanent Glass Flowers gallery itself opened to the public on 17 April 1893; modern renovations also explicitly include vibration control. Those later facts are context only. They do not explain the Chicago event and should not be used anachronistically as evidence for its mechanism.

### Scope guard

This Harvard Ware Collection exhibition event must remain separate from the Field Museum / Ward zoological-Blaschka material at the 1893 fair already represented elsewhere in the global-object backend. They are distinct object histories.

## 7. Revised clock architecture

The project now supports at least seven analytically distinct clocks:

1. **production clock** — when a model is made/finished;
2. **dispatch clock** — mounting, crate manufacture, packing, insurance, consular documentation;
3. **transport/clearance clock** — carrier, bond, customs, broker and museum delivery;
4. **settlement/accounting clock** — when distributed charges are gathered, corrected and paid;
5. **reference clock** — when specimens, drawings, seeds, books or living material become available;
6. **shipment/counting clock** — when heterogeneous work is aggregated into an institutional consignment of a negotiated size;
7. **installation/exhibition clock** — when received models become stable in display, or become mobile/damaged again and require repair.

These clocks need not synchronize. A model can be production-complete but not dispatchable; physically present but not customs-released; installed but mechanically unstable; paid for while older inspector carriage remains outstanding.

This makes `different clocks` a documentable system rather than a metaphor.

## 8. Revised first-order argument

The strongest current formulation is now:

> **The Blaschka workshop and its objects remained mobile beyond the voyage itself. Transport consisted of successive regimes of making things dispatchable, legally movable, financially settleable and physically stable. Each regime could reopen the object: customs could require inspection, invoices could reconstruct an earlier route, and exhibition supports could remobilize an object that had already arrived intact.**

A second formulation is useful for the institutional side:

> **Transport infrastructure was also memory infrastructure.**

Ehrhorn, Emden & Mayer stored route knowledge on the Bremen side; Snow accumulated the receiving/customs routine in Boston; Goodale preserved bills and memoranda; later correspondents reused those names and arrangements. The 1906 and 1923 Snow failures show that this accumulated memory could become obsolete while still shaping action.
