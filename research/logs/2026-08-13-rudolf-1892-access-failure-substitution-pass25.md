# Rudolf 1892 access failure / substitution repertoire pass 25 — 13 August 2026

## Purpose

Pass 24 established a necessary control: seeds, roots, cultivated American plants, detached fresh material and books were already feeding Hosterwitz before Rudolf travelled. The 1892 journey was designed around what the existing supply ecology still left `hitherto inaccessible`.

The next question is what happened when field presence itself failed to deliver the desired reference. The answer is consistent across several otherwise different cases. The expedition did not treat failed access as the end of the chain. Actors changed site, time, medium, supplier or target. In some cases they abandoned the reference altogether.

The strongest formulation after this pass is:

> **The 1892 expedition repeatedly converted access failures into alternative reference pathways. The productive operation was often not direct collecting but substitution, deferral, relay, remote decision or rejection.**

This pushes the paper further away from an itinerary narrative. A place where Rudolf did not go, a flower he did not see, or a plant he did not collect can be just as analytically important as a successful field encounter if the failure reorganized the reference chain.

No public map changes are made.

## 1. Sequoia: habitat access succeeds while phenological access fails

In the San Francisco/Oakland letter `BLA-D00856 / 017:210`, Rudolf describes the day trip to the giant redwoods near Santa Cruz. The trees are encountered directly. He measures one, finds useful plants in the forest, and enjoys seeing wild forms related to plants grown in the Hosterwitz garden.

The desired *Sequoia sempervirens* flowers, however, are unavailable because the flowering period of most central Californian conifers is already over.

This is a useful distinction:

`access to habitat ≠ access to required plant state`.

The repair strategy is temporal and social. Rudolf expects acquaintances among local botanists to send flowers later.

The expedition has therefore created a supplier relation in place of a completed observation. The missing flower is deferred beyond the visit.

The later delivery remains open and should stay open until a follow-up letter identifies the actual material.

## 2. Darlingtonia: expensive habitat access is replaced by cultivated institutional access

Darlingtonia is the clearest substitution case and remains governed by the source-critical closure established in pass 05.

On 18 May (`BLA-D00856 / 017:210`), Rudolf says Goodale had emphasized *Darlingtonia californica* before departure because the plant did not flower in the greenhouse conditions available to him. Reaching its Shasta-area habitat would take roughly four days and cost $70. Ganong proposes telegraphing Goodale before committing the time and money.

The later Colorado Springs follow-up (`BLA-D00873–00874 / 017:227–228`) closes the route decision: after the travellers had continued east, Goodale's telegraphic reply reached them saying that they **should not go to Shasta**.

Days later in St Louis (`BLA-D00896 / 017:251`), the target reappears in a different access regime. At the botanical garden, Darlingtonia is in bloom. Rudolf receives a workroom, makes a colour sketch and puts a specimen in alcohol.

The chain is therefore:

`desired plant → costly habitat proposal → telegraph → habitat leg cancelled → cultivated flowering plant in St Louis → sketch + preserved specimen`.

A botanical garden substitutes for the proposed habitat journey.

The English translation of `BLA-D00896` contains the contradictory clause that Darlingtonia was the plant “because of which we travelled to the Shasta Mountains.” The explicit telegram and subsequent eastbound route narrative are stronger itinerary evidence. Keep Shasta as a proposed/cancelled leg unless the German image overturns that sequence.

## 3. Crinum and Orcutt: missed flowering becomes a procurement programme

The Cambridge letter `BLA-D00927 / 017:282` shows a different repair strategy after the western journey is effectively over.

Rudolf reports receiving a large *Crinum* bulb from Orcutt in San Diego. It follows an earlier seed packet from the same supplier. Rudolf then proposes a continuing arrangement with Orcutt for larger quantities of seeds and bulbs of plants whose flowering time he and Ganong had been unable to wait for in California.

The field-season limit does not become a simple negative result. It produces a later supply channel:

`missed flowering during visit → local supplier → seed/bulb dispatch after traveller leaves → future cultivation/study`.

This is a stronger mechanism than saying that Orcutt “helped” the expedition. He turns phenological delay into asynchronous procurement.

## 4. Living cacti: change the medium so the missing state can occur later

The cactus packet reconstructed in passes 21–22 supplies another type of repair.

At Tempe Rudolf digs up young plants after drawing cactus flowers and explicitly says they need not be preserved in alcohol. In California the Arizona cacti are joined by other material. On 4 May a small box of cacti and flowering bulbs is sent to Cambridge for intended onward forwarding to Hosterwitz. A later letter says that living cacti are preferable because the workshop can grow the flower itself.

Here the problem is not an inaccessible place but an incompletely available biological state.

The solution is a change in reference medium:

`preserved body → living organism capable of later flowering`.

Transport carries the possibility of a future observation.

The Hosterwitz receipt/survival of this particular box remains unclosed.

## 5. Cambridge duplicates: botanical access can succeed and still be institutionally useless

The Colorado Springs letter `BLA-D00879 / 017:233` adds another kind of failure. Rudolf says that the people in Cambridge are “picky”: they do not want this or that species because it is already represented.

This is important because it separates botanical availability from collection value.

A plant can be:

- present;
- in flower;
- identifiable;
- drawable;

and still be rejected because Harvard's existing collection makes it redundant.

So usefulness is relational to the commission and collection state, not an intrinsic quality of a plant encountered in the field.

The individual rejected taxa are not yet identified.

## 6. Tiny plants and the Jumping Cactus: deliberate noncollection is part of production

The Tempe letter `BLA-D00785 / 017:139` provides the sharpest negative selection statement.

Rudolf says that many desert plants were very small and that most were ignored. The party concentrated on the showiest and most characteristic forms. He also refuses to collect the dangerous `Jumping Cactus` because of its hooked spines.

This is not a breakdown of the collecting system. It is one way the system remains workable.

`encounter → reject` is therefore a legitimate terminal branch in the reference-production model.

Material danger, size and representational value can stop a plant before it enters any transport chain.

---

## 7. The access-repair repertoire

Across these cases, six distinct operations are visible:

1. **substitute site** — Darlingtonia: St Louis cultivated material replaces the Shasta habitat leg;
2. **defer in time** — Sequoia flowers and California flowering plants are to arrive later;
3. **change medium** — selected cacti remain alive rather than becoming alcohol preparations;
4. **delegate access** — Orcutt and local botanists become later suppliers;
5. **remote-govern route** — Goodale's telegram helps decide whether expensive field access is worth pursuing;
6. **reject** — duplicates, tiny plants and dangerous material are excluded.

These categories should remain operational rather than theoretical. They describe what actors actually do when the preferred reference is unavailable or not worth obtaining.

---

## 8. Why this changes the meaning of fieldwork

Pass 24 already showed that the 1892 journey did not replace remote supply. Pass 25 shows something stronger: even while travelling, Rudolf repeatedly returns to remote supply, cultivated access and delegated procurement.

The field is therefore not the opposite of the network. It is a place from which new networks are generated.

A failed flower in California can produce a later supplier relation. A costly proposed habitat can produce a telegram and an institutional substitute. A living plant can be moved so that the relevant observation occurs in Saxony months later.

Direct presence is one resource among several, not the universal endpoint.

---

## 9. Paper-level consequence

The paper can now avoid two weak formulations:

- `Rudolf travelled because seeing plants in nature was more authentic`;
- `transport networks simply enabled him to bring specimens home`.

The archive supports a more exact problem:

> **How did a commissioned modelling project keep producing usable references when the desired plant, flower, place or season was unavailable?**

The answer is a repertoire of access repair. Different failures produce different replacements, and those replacements leave traces in telegrams, botanical gardens, seed packets, live plants, alcohol specimens, supplier relations and explicit acts of rejection.

That is both falsifiable and visible at event level.

---

## 10. Map decision

Do not represent failed access as missing route data.

Darlingtonia/Shasta, Sequoia's missed flowers and Orcutt's later supply are analytically meaningful because the expected route or observation **did not occur**.

A future detail-layer object could therefore show:

`desired target → access problem → substitute / defer / change medium / delegate / reject → resulting reference`.

This is better than adding more route lines.
