# Southwick transatlantic packing capability + firm chronology — pass 40 — 13 August 2026

## Purpose

Passes 36–39 established the direct 1890–1891 Blaschka/Southwick connection and kept the personal identity of the `Horsford` partner unresolved. This pass asks two narrower questions: what can be said about the Southwick firm's actual commercial transport capability, and how securely can the `Gillett & Horsford` business form be dated?

## Result

A directly relevant transport statement has now surfaced from the firm's 1895 catalogue, *Our little book of wild flowers and hardy ornamentals, evergreen ferns, shrubs, new tigridias, etc.* The catalogue advises express shipment where distance is not too great because greater care can be taken in packing. It then states that small orders can be sent safely by mail and, with the firm's packing method, can reach their destination **even in Europe** in good condition.

This is not proof of the 1890 Fritillaria shipment to Hosterwitz. It is, however, direct longitudinal evidence that the same Southwick firm publicly represented itself as capable of packing living plant orders for successful European delivery. That makes the Blaschka workshop's 1891 statement about a California bulb previously sent by `Gillett & Horsford, Southwick Mass.` materially intelligible as part of an established commercial practice rather than as an anomalous transatlantic favour.

## 1. Transport mechanism: carrier choice and packing are coupled

The 1895 catalogue distinguishes two regimes:

- **express**, preferred where distance permits because more care can be taken in packing and extra plants may be added;
- **mail**, suitable for small orders, with a packing method claimed to keep them in good condition even to Europe.

The useful point is operational. Nursery transport was not simply `plant → post`. Carrier choice, order size and packing technique were treated together. The commercial object was prepared differently according to the route and scale of shipment.

For the Blaschka reference-supply ecology this gives a counterpart mechanism to the workshop-side evidence:

`desired living reference → nursery stock → order size / carrier choice → packing method → long-distance survival → Hosterwitz cultivation → observation / modelling`.

The last steps are directly documented in the 1891 Fritillaria letter; the 1895 catalogue supplies the nursery-side transport logic at firm level.

Guard: do not project the exact 1895 packing method backward onto the specific 1890 bulb without an invoice, letter or earlier catalogue wording.

## 2. The 1890 catalogue is securely a Gillett & Horsford/Southwick object

Wikimedia Commons and BHL expose a 36-page PDF titled `Gillett & Horsford's 1890 descriptive catalogue of wild flowers, shrubs, trees and vines`, Internet Archive identifier `gilletthorsfords1890sout`. BHL's bibliography records Southwick Nurseries, Southwick, Massachusetts, 1890.

A second 1890 Southwick catalogue is explicitly titled `Southwick Nurseries 1890 wholesale catalogue of North American perennials, new Tigridias, California bulbs, etc.` The category `California bulbs` is therefore contemporaneous with the year in which the Blaschkas later said their California Fritillaria bulb had been sent from Southwick.

This is strong commercial-context evidence, but a catalogue category is not a transaction. No taxon-level hit for *Fritillaria recurva* has yet been recovered from searchable metadata/snippets, and the PDF itself still needs page-level inspection.

## 3. The firm's chronology can be bounded, but not yet legally reconstructed

Current catalog/archival metadata gives the following working sequence:

- **1890** — BHL/Commons: `Gillett & Horsford's 1890 descriptive catalogue...`; Southwick Nurseries.
- **1891** — Harvard HOLLIS: `Gillett and Horsford (Edward Gillett), 1891`; surviving Gillett letters use `Southwick Nurseries (Gillett & Horsford)` business stationery.
- **1892** — BHL/NAL catalogues continue under Southwick Nurseries; metadata may suppress personal/corporate author detail.
- **1895** — NAL/AGRIS: corporate author/publisher `Gillett & Horsford`, Southwick Nurseries; catalogue directly states mail packing can reach Europe in good condition.
- **1896** — NAL/AGRIS `Wholesale trade list`: Southwick Nurseries; note `Edward Gillett, Southwick Nurseries`.
- **1897 onward** — catalogues are explicitly attributed/published as Edward Gillett / Edward Gillett (Firm), Southwick.

This bounds a visible `Gillett & Horsford` commercial form across at least 1890–1895 and an Edward-Gillett-only bibliographic form by 1896–97. It does **not** establish partnership formation/dissolution dates or the identity of the partner.

Bibliographic metadata can normalize names retrospectively, so direct stationery and title-page evidence outrank catalogue authority fields when chronology conflicts.

## 4. A spelling variant must be retained

The May 1890 *Popular Science Monthly* `Publications Received` list records:

`Southwick Nurseries, Massachusetts. Gillett & Hosford. Catalogue of Wild Flowers, Shrubs, Trees, and Vines.`

The relevant Wikisource page is proofread but not fully validated. BHL/Commons and Harvard archival descriptions use `Horsford`. Therefore `Hosford` is retained as a contemporary printed/transcription variant or possible error, not silently normalized into evidence about partner identity.

This variant is worth keeping because future directory or newspaper searches should query **both `Horsford` and `Hosford`**.

## 5. Frederick Hinsdale Horsford remains separate

Independent current-history and BHL evidence describes Frederick Hinsdale Horsford and Cyrus G. Pringle as nursery partners from 1883; F. H. Horsford bought out Pringle in 1893 and thereafter operated his Charlotte, Vermont nursery. BHL catalogues independently show an F.H. Horsford firm at Charlotte in 1893–95.

That chronology makes Frederick a contemporaneous nursery proprietor during the Southwick partnership period, but still supplies no explicit Southwick bridge. He may or may not be the same `Horsford`. The `do_not_merge` guard from pass 39 therefore stands.

## 6. Source hierarchy for the Southwick actor/firm problem

Use this hierarchy:

1. **direct workshop transaction statement** — Blaschka 19 Jul 1891 naming Gillett & Horsford, Southwick;
2. **contemporaneous business stationery / invoice / catalogue title page**;
3. **Harvard archival folder description**;
4. **contemporaneous publication notice/review**;
5. **NAL/BHL bibliographic corporate-author metadata**;
6. **later nursery histories / local history**.

Do not allow a lower layer to overwrite a higher one without explicit reconciliation.

## 7. Research consequence

The pre-1892 supply ecology now has a firmer logistics component. The workshop evidence says a California bulb crossed from Southwick to Hosterwitz and flowered there. The firm's later catalogue says its packing system was designed to deliver small living-plant orders successfully even to Europe. The history can therefore be posed as a problem of **reference survival across commercial horticultural transport**, not merely botanical procurement.

A useful formulation:

> **Before Rudolf travelled to America, part of America could already reach his workshop as commercially packed living stock. The epistemic problem began again after arrival: whether the plant survived, flowered in the needed state, matched published descriptions and could be identified well enough to model.**

This statement should always preserve the chronology: European-mail packing is directly documented in 1895; the specific Southwick→Hosterwitz Fritillaria shipment is directly documented retrospectively in the workshop letter of 1891 as having occurred the previous year.
