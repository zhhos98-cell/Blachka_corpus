# Rudolf 1892 collection-aware filtering pass 29 — 13 August 2026

## Result

Pass 28 established that expedition quantities belong to different media and counting regimes. The next question is earlier in the chain: **what determined whether a plant entered the reference stock at all?**

`BLA-D00879 / 017:233`, Rudolf's Colorado Springs letter of 24 May 1892, gives the strongest answer yet. After describing new material from the canyon and his current drawing pace, Rudolf warns his parents against imagining that faster work would automatically yield many more species. He names two independent limits. First, the season does not offer an unlimited selection. Second, **Cambridge is selective because it does not want species that are already represented**. He then says that reaching 600–800 species would require spending an entire year wandering around America, something he does not want to do. At that point he reports 200 species drawn and studied and projects perhaps 400 by departure.

This changes the selection model. Harvard was not simply a receiving institution downstream of fieldwork. Its existing collection entered the field as a **remote negative selector**.

> **Field selection was inventory-aware. A plant could be locally available and worth noticing yet fail to become a retained reference because Cambridge already possessed the representational category it needed.**

No public map change.

---

## 1. The 200/400 numbers already contain filtering

The 24 May letter is especially valuable because several operations appear in one paragraph:

- Rudolf is finding flowering material in Colorado;
- he is drawing/studying it at a rate constrained by his health and desired pace;
- seasonal availability constrains what exists to be worked on;
- Cambridge's existing holdings constrain what is institutionally worth adding;
- he projects a possible final yield while explicitly rejecting the time required for a much larger total.

The usual temptation is to read `200` and `400` as simple accumulation. They are closer to **post-filter working counts**. The source tells us that the potential field universe had already been reduced by season, institutional redundancy and Rudolf's own bodily/time limits before those numbers were generated.

A useful operational formula is therefore:

`field reference yield = availability × maker selection × collection gap × media feasibility × body/time/route capacity`.

This is not a mathematical claim. It is a checklist of gates that prevents the archive's numbers from being treated as one undifferentiated species count.

---

## 2. Cambridge reaches upstream into the field

The historiographically useful point is the direction of influence. We have often treated the route as producing references that Cambridge later receives, sorts and displays. `BLA-D00879` shows collection management acting before return.

The existing Harvard collection supplies a negative criterion: `already represented`.

That criterion can change the value of a plant without changing the plant. Two flowering species in the same locality can occupy different positions in the expedition because one fills a collection gap while another duplicates an existing representation.

This makes `selection` bilateral in a precise sense:

- Rudolf selects locally according to form, usefulness, accessibility and what he can study;
- Cambridge's collection state supplies information about redundancy and need.

The result is not a field collection subsequently curated by Harvard. It is field production already conditioned by a distant collection.

---

## 3. This also explains why botanical gardens alter throughput

The same letter says work in a good botanical garden is easier because names and other information are already at hand. Earlier parts of the route forced Rudolf to obtain material from the wild and often identify or contextualize it through other means.

Botanical-garden access therefore reduces one cost in the selection chain: identification/search effort. Cambridge collection-state filtering reduces another kind of uncertainty: whether another representation is useful to the commission.

These are different infrastructures. One says **what this plant is**; the other helps determine **whether Harvard needs another representation of it**.

St Louis becomes important partly because cultivated, identified material compresses the work needed to turn an encounter into a usable reference. Darlingtonia is the strongest object-level case of that substitution mechanism.

---

## 4. Negative evidence has to be handled more strictly

At present we have a direct statement that some species were unwanted because already represented. We do **not** yet have a reliable list of which species Rudolf had proposed and Cambridge rejected on that ground.

That matters. Several tempting shortcuts would manufacture evidence:

- absence from later model lists does not prove Cambridge rejected a plant;
- presence of another species in the same genus does not prove redundancy;
- a herbarium sheet without a model descendant does not prove that glass production was ever proposed;
- a model that predates 1892 does not by itself prove Rudolf encountered and rejected the same species during the trip.

The correct next threshold is a named decision event. Ideally:

`field taxon named → reference proposed or discussed → Cambridge says already represented / unnecessary → Rudolf stops, changes medium or redirects effort`.

Until that appears, `already represented` remains a directly attested **mechanism**, not a species-level rejection register.

---

## 5. Where to look for named rejections

The Harvard finding-aid layer points to a better source class than another generic corpus search: **Acquisition, Development, and Shipping lists**, including files covering 1892, alongside Goodale/Ware correspondence and model-label material.

Those records may allow three things to be joined:

1. the collection state before/during the expedition;
2. taxa under consideration in letters or lists;
3. explicit decisions about duplication, desired gaps or shipment priority.

Goodale's correspondence is equally important because the Darlingtonia telegram already proves that Cambridge could intervene remotely in field decisions. A letter naming an already-represented taxon would turn the generic 24 May statement into an event-level case.

---

## 6. Revised two-stage selective-conversion model

Pass 19 used `selective conversion` mainly for the gap between a broad field reference packet and a smaller set of later glass descendants. Pass 29 now requires two distinct filters.

### Gate A: entry into the reference stock

`encounter → local/maker selection → Cambridge collection-gap test → medium assignment → retained reference`

### Gate B: later workshop activation

`retained reference → production priority / season / available supporting material / workshop capacity → glass model`

A taxon can disappear at either gate. That is analytically important because `was encountered but never modelled` and `was retained as a reference but never modelled` are different histories.

Hoffmannseggia currently passes both gates. Several Tempe herbarium sheets clearly pass Gate A but have no secure Gate B result yet. We currently lack a named plant that is demonstrably stopped by Cambridge at Gate A.

---

## 7. Article consequence

The paper can now make a more exact claim about distributed selection:

> **The travelling maker did not carry an open-ended collecting mandate. His field productivity was governed against a distant and changing collection. Harvard's existing representations could make a newly encountered plant redundant before it ever became a workshop reference.**

This helps separate the argument from a general story of mobility or collecting. Transport is tied to **collection state**: what should move depends partly on what has already moved and survived before it.

In that sense, the museum collection acts as memory infrastructure. Previous shipments alter the value of future field encounters.
