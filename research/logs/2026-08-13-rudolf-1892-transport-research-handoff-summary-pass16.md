# Rudolf 1892 transport research handoff summary — pass 16 — 13 August 2026

## Purpose

Consolidate the transport investigation before the next research session. This file is a handoff, not a new public-map pass. It records the strongest findings now supported across the active Blaschka backend, the 1892 research files, GitHub logistics registers and external archival/web calibration.

The project has moved beyond a simple `person route + object route` comparison. The current model is a **multiplex transport system** in which people, finished glass, specimens, drawings, seeds, books, documents, money and institutional authority move on different routes and clocks.

The strongest first-order formulation at this point is:

> **1892 split the Blaschka workshop across stationary production, travelling observation and multiple material packets. Transport kept the split workshop coupled by converting plants, models and information into differently movable, handleable, inspectable, documentable, countable and financially settleable forms.**

A second formulation is equally important:

> **Arrival was not the end of transport.**

A Blaschka shipment could continue through customs release, broker settlement, unpacking, installation, vibration, breakage, repair, remounting and administrative memory.

## 1. Core source correction: separate shipment, traveller and repair events

The old year-level cluster `CONS-1892-01` over-compressed several distinct episodes.

### 1.1 January 1892 shipment

`BLA-D00016 / 015:16` states that U.B. 346–350 were to leave Hosterwitz by railway freight on 21 January 1892. The five cases contained:

- 60 plant models;
- 329 analytical details;
- Bremen forwarding;
- New York destination;
- onward movement `in Bond to Boston`.

The finishing work on this unusually large consignment delayed Rudolf's own departure.

The annotated Harvard shipment list now securely crosswalks this sixth shipment to **model nos. 252–311 inclusive = exactly 60 numbered models**. Model 251 is before the sixth-shipment heading; model 312 begins the seventh shipment.

### 1.2 Rudolf's passenger movement

Rudolf travelled separately through passenger infrastructure and underwent a distinct New York passenger-customs event. His own later account distinguishes his arrival from that of the five freight cases.

The key analytical fact is that the same workshop released a maker and a finished-glass consignment into **two different transport regimes**.

### 1.3 June 1892 repair

Goodale's June repair letters should not be attributed directly to U.B. 346–350. February receiving evidence says the opened sample from the current shipment was in excellent condition. The June letters refer to badly broken pieces from the `first consignment` and attribute the damage to the New York custom house.

Therefore maintain three separate events:

1. January-February 1892 U.B. 346–350 shipment and receiving sequence;
2. Rudolf's separate outbound passenger journey;
3. June 1892 repair of earlier/accumulated customs damage.

Do not re-collapse them.

## 2. The 1892 shipment is now reconstructable as a handoff chain

The strongest current chain is:

`Hosterwitz workshop → railway freight → Bremen forwarding agent → Atlantic freight → New York bonded movement → Boston receiving/customs intermediary → Harvard Botanical Museum → authorized opening/unpacking`

Important actors and institutional roles include:

- Leopold and Rudolf Blaschka as sender/manufacturing side;
- Ehrhorn, Emden & Mayer on the Bremen side;
- ocean carrier;
- bonded customs system;
- E. A. Snow on the Boston receiving/clearance side;
- Capt./Col. Wales of the Custom House;
- Goodale, Ganong and museum staff at Harvard.

The exact New York→Boston carrier for the 1892 five cases remains unresolved and must not be inferred from the 1893 `Ocean and Sound Steamers` bill.

## 3. February 1892 receiving sequence: arrival is staged

The five cases did not simply `arrive at Harvard` in one event.

The correspondence distinguishes:

1. advice/papers reaching Goodale;
2. papers handed to E. A. Snow;
3. physical cases reaching the museum;
4. outer handling and lifting to the museum floor;
5. preliminary opening;
6. later formal customs inspection;
7. fuller unpacking and museum use.

`BLA-D00695 / 017:49` is especially important because the same letter records two opposite material flows:

- roughly 29 species of Cambridge Botanic Garden seeds sent from Cambridge to Hosterwitz, with cultivation instructions;
- the five Blaschka cases arriving at Harvard.

This is the cleanest current same-page **counterflow** scene: finished glass westbound, seed/reference material eastbound.

The same receiving letter shows that Harvard already possessed distributed handling expertise. Museum staff removed outer packing; Goodale opened a case and removed *Datura arborea*; later customs inspection involved Snow and Wales. Do not reduce safe unpacking to Rudolf's embodied knowledge alone.

Working term: **distributed handling knowledge**.

## 4. Object-state transformation during transport

Transport should no longer be defined only as point-to-point movement.

A finished glass model could pass through states such as:

`finished representation → mounted/handleable transport unit → inner box/carton → outer case → bonded/customs object → opened/inspected object → remounted museum object → installed exhibition object`

The 1893 correspondence already says fragile models were fixed on glass panes because otherwise they could not be safely handled. The later technical transport method makes the sequence even more explicit through cardboard supports, tissue immobilisation, wooden cases, straw/burlap, customs opening and final gypsum remounting.

Thus **representational completion did not equal logistical readiness**.

A distinct **dispatch clock** begins after production and includes mounting, crate manufacture, packing, insurance, consular certification and forwarder readiness.

## 5. Split workshop: Rudolf travelling, Leopold producing

The Harvard annotated shipment list identifies model nos. **312–322** as made by Leopold in the first half of 1892 while Rudolf was away.

Rudolf's 1900 retrospective correspondence independently corroborates this eleven-model block and describes his father's continued production while he travelled.

These models were not shipped until the seventh shipment in February 1893.

Therefore:

- workshop production continued at Hosterwitz during Rudolf's expedition;
- production clock and shipment clock diverged;
- 1892 is better described as a **split workshop** than a workshop that simply became mobile.

Current preferred formulation:

> **1892 did not mobilize a workshop. It split one. Transport then kept the split workshop coupled.**

## 6. Shipment as aggregation technology

The seventh shipment demonstrates that a numbered consignment can combine objects with different histories.

It contains at least:

- Leopold's first-half-1892 stationary production;
- later cryptogamic/anatomical work;
- some American plants derived from Rudolf's expedition.

The eighth shipment is even more explicit. The 18 July 1893 letter divides 37 ordinary plant models into:

- 20 after Jamaica studies;
- 15 after western studies;
- 2 after garden material;

followed by a separate higher-science/cryptogamic block.

The annotated list gives 374–410 = exactly 37 numbered ordinary-plant models, followed by scientific models 411–423. The current high-confidence sequence reconstruction is:

- 374–393 Jamaica = 20;
- 394–408 West = 15;
- 409–410 garden = 2.

Publication-level use still requires original-list-image checking because the OCR does not print those regional subheadings.

The ninth-shipment boundary is now corrected and closed:

- January 1894 ninth shipment = **424–473 = 50 models**;
- model 474 starts the tenth shipment.

The tenth shipment = **474–523 = 50 models**, physically shipped in four cases I.B. 366–369 in August 1894.

Thus `shipment` is not a production-time unit. It is an **aggregation event** that compresses heterogeneous production histories into a periodic institutional consignment.

## 7. Representational divergence: model 433 Hoffmannseggia

Model 433 is currently the strongest specimen/model microhistory.

The Harvard University Herbaria database preserves an 1892 Tempe specimen collected by W. F. Ganong and R. Blaschka, GH 01676890, now identified as *Hoffmannseggia glauca*.

Kew synonymy connects historical *Hoffmannseggia stricta* / *H. densiflora* with the accepted *H. glauca*.

Harvard Glass Flowers documentation identifies model 433 under the corresponding historical/updated nomenclature, and Rudolf's 1900 authorship letter names 433 among models he retrospectively claimed to have constructed himself, while qualifying the role of Leopold in analytical details.

The analytical sequence is therefore approximately:

`living Arizona plant / field encounter → herbarium specimen retained at Harvard`

and separately:

`field reference lineage → Hosterwitz manufacture → model 433 → January 1894 shipment → Harvard`

This is **representational divergence**, not a simple specimen-becomes-model sequence. One descendant remains a herbarium sheet; another becomes glass.

Publication guard: inspect the original digitized model-433 label and specimen-sheet image/annotations before presenting this as a fully closed one-to-one modelling-source relation.

## 8. Negative controls matter

Other Tempe specimens collected by Ganong and Rudolf survive at Harvard without a secure model crosswalk in the current corpus, including *Daucus pusillus*, *Cryptantha maritima* and *Brassica nigra*.

These are important controls against outcome bias. The expedition produced a reference archive with multiple afterlives. Not every collected specimen should be assumed to have become a Glass Flower.

This supports **selective / partial reassembly** rather than total reassembly at Hosterwitz.

## 9. Transport media preserve different futures

The 1892 correspondence distinguishes several material forms:

- living plants;
- alcohol specimens;
- dried/herbarium specimens;
- drawings and colour studies;
- seeds;
- bulbs/cuttings.

Rudolf explicitly says living cacti can be preferable to alcohol-preserved ones because the workshop can grow the flower itself.

The key question is therefore:

> **What had to remain possible after transport?**

Different media preserve different future affordances. Alcohol may stabilize morphology; living transport may preserve future flowering; seeds preserve deferred growth; drawings preserve colour/form relationships; herbarium sheets preserve another class of diagnostic evidence.

Transport requirements therefore help determine the form in which representation can exist.

## 10. Borrowed commercial infrastructure: Boston Fruit Company in Jamaica

Rudolf's Jamaican journey used Boston Fruit Company steamship infrastructure.

Direct correspondence names the *Bowden* for the outbound Jamaica passage and later describes fruit-shipping operations shaping coastal movement, waiting time, luggage transfer and local assistance. Boston Fruit Company personnel helped solve lodging, luggage/ground-transport and postal problems.

On the return sequence, the fruit carrier's need to gather its banana cargo across Jamaican ports structured the passenger itinerary.

Current strongest subordinate claim:

> **The scientific itinerary was partly produced by the cargo itinerary.**

Do not reduce this to a loose `commerce enabled science` formulation. The stronger mechanism is that a commercial system optimized for another perishable commodity imposed its own timetable, port sequence and local intermediaries on the scientific traveller.

A major counterpart archive exists at Baker Library, Harvard Business School:

- Boston Fruit Company letterpress copybook, 16 Sep 1891–9 Nov 1894;
- private ledger No. I, 1892–1898.

These are major next-session retrieval targets.

## 11. E. A. Snow: receiving infrastructure and procedural memory

Snow now has a longitudinal history from 1892 through 1923.

Secure primary evidence establishes:

- 1893: E. A. Snow, 42 Summer Street, Boston;
- 1906: Goodale calls him formerly a Custom-house Broker on State Street and says, `He always did our work well`;
- 1923: Rudolf still routes cases to him as `Customhouse broker and forwarding agent`.

Snow's function across the archive includes:

- receiving papers;
- customs/broker coordination;
- sending or coordinating inspectors;
- freight and express settlement;
- inspector carriage;
- commission;
- museum delivery/clearance routine.

Do not guess his first name or a corporate identity from generic directory matches.

### Snow as path dependence

In 1906 Harvard discovered Snow was no longer in business even though goods were still consigned to him.

In 1923 Rudolf again routed cases through Snow; Bremen agents later learned that E. A. Snow `does no more exist`, had to seek another American agent, and the failure probably caused delay.

Strong formulation:

> **A durable route was partly a durable memory of whom to address.**

Transport infrastructure was also memory infrastructure.

## 13. Settlement/accounting clock

The September 1893 correspondence says Snow billed all freight and express charges from Hosterwitz to Cambridge plus inspector costs, but also carried forward a carriage charge from Col. Wales's previous visit that had been omitted from an earlier bill.

Goodale sent the memoranda to Ware `for preservation`.

This establishes:

- physical arrival does not close the transport event;
- omitted charges can remain open and appear in a later account;
- accounts carry inter-shipment administrative memory;
- transport bills themselves become archival objects.

Add a distinct **settlement/accounting clock** to the model.

---

## 14. Chicago 1893: display is another mobility regime

The Chicago World's Columbian Exposition correspondence provides a strong post-arrival control.

Initial express transport appears to have succeeded with only limited breakage. But Goodale later reports that the velvet intended to keep the models safe had acted as a `machine for moving them in all directions`; specimens had shifted and many were broken in the exhibition setting.

He then repaired, remounted, relabelled/recovered the display and stress-tested the new arrangement before reopening it.

The strongest operational sequence is:

`express carriage → installation → renewed movement/vibration → breakage → repair → remounting → testing → public display`

Thus **installation does not terminate mobility**.

The velvet episode should be paired conceptually with later packing-straw cases:

- a material can be mechanically protective in one regime and become a regulatory liability in another;
- a material intended to immobilize can become a mobility-producing material under exhibition conditions.

Do not overstate the exact vibration source until BLA-D00149–150 are image-checked.

## 15. Seven-clock architecture

The project now supports at least seven distinct clocks:

1. **production clock** — when a model is made/finished;
2. **dispatch clock** — when mounting, box-making, packing, insurance and certification make it dispatchable;
3. **transport/clearance clock** — when carriers, bond, customs, broker and museum receipt place it institutionally;
4. **settlement/accounting clock** — when freight/customs/inspector/commission charges are reconstructed and closed;
5. **reference clock** — when specimens, drawings, seeds, living materials and books become available;
6. **shipment/counting clock** — when heterogeneous work is aggregated into a numbered consignment of an expected/negotiated size;
7. **installation/exhibition clock** — when the received object becomes stable in display or becomes mobile/damaged again.

The strongest current conceptual sentence is:

> **The same object inhabited different clocks.**

A model could be made but not dispatchable; arrived but not released; released but not financially settled; installed but not mechanically stable.

## 16. Accounting and valuation

The 1890 exclusive contract specifies 8,800 marks per year in two 4,400-mark half-year instalments, two shipments per year, and Harvard responsibility for expenses from place of manufacture to Cambridge including insurance and consular certificate.

The August 1894 workshop account separately lists:

- Modelle 4240 M;
- 4 Kisten 50 M;
- Cartons 55 M;
- Emballieren 30.30 M;
- Transport nach Dresden 11.50 M;
- Versicherung bis Bremen 2.50 M;
- Consular Beglaubigung 10.70 M;
- total 4400 M.

The 4,400 total strikingly matches the half-year instalment, but do **not** yet call 4240 a production price or 4400 a sale price. The contract says transport/insurance/consular expenses are separately defrayed by Harvard, so the notebook may represent invoice/consular/accounting translation rather than simple sale value.

Safe conclusion:

> **The archive separates model valuation, cases, cartons, packing labour, local transport, insurance and consular certification as distinct accounting categories.**

Matching Harvard-side invoices remain a priority retrieval target.
