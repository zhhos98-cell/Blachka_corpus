# Source infrastructure pass: logistics, packing, customs and payment — 10 August 2026

## Purpose

This pass extends `/sources/` beyond maker, dealer and museum archives. The source problem is now organised around the institutions and document genres that made circulation possible: forwarding agents, ocean carriers, customs authorities, brokers, workshop packing records, receiving technicians, banks, lawyers and exchange-control authorities.

The working chain is:

`workshop → dealer → forwarder → carrier → customs/quarantine → broker/payment intermediary → museum → unpacking/repair`

Each role is kept distinct. A company archive is not treated as evidence for a Blaschka shipment until a transaction can be matched by date, case mark, consignee, account, vessel or named actor.

## Ehrhorn, Emden & Mayer

The Bremen forwarding firm is no longer a one-shipment detail. Dated workshop evidence now shows a relationship from at least 1891 to 1929:

- 1891: five large cases to Bremen/Bremerhaven for New York; the firm sends shipping advice.
- 1892: Rudolf calls the firm `our agent`; it provides travel documents for his own American journey and connects him to Norddeutscher Lloyd.
- 1906: the firm forwards an insured Harvard shipment onto the next North German Lloyd steamer; a consular invoice accompanies the consignment.
- 1922: Rudolf calls them `our old forwarding agents` and seeks advice because new export rules threaten his requirement that the models not be repacked.
- 1923: the firm arranges the New York steamer before the four R.B. cases move in bond to Boston and into the E. A. Snow customs-broker chain.
- 1929: the firm proposes a cheaper direct-Boston route. Rudolf rejects it because changing route would require new consular paperwork and delay, then reports the cases loaded on `President Harding` for New York.

Rudolf's 1929 statement that the New York-in-bond-to-Boston route had been used since 1888 is retained as a retrospective claim, not direct 1888 proof. No dedicated Ehrhorn, Emden & Mayer corporate fonds surfaced in the public-catalogue pass; the next route is Bremen commercial registration and address-book infrastructure rather than another generic web sweep.

## Packing as a feedback system

`packing-feedback-unpacking-register.json` separates packing from unpacking and records the receiving side as part of the technique.

The 1930 `Method of preparing the models for Transportation` describes: firm cardboard support; strong cardboard box; crushed tissue paper to immobilise dependent parts; pinewood outer box; straw between inner boxes; an outer bale of straw and burlap; customs opening in the Museum; removal from transport supports; remounting onto plaster plaques; labelling and display.

Oakes Ames's 15 December 1930 letter feeds receiver knowledge back into this procedure. He recommends tissue-paper support because vibration persists in transit and warns that inspectors are strict about plant material used for packing.

The September–October 1932 correspondence supplies the receiving-skill control. Harvard reports three cases and twenty-one models with only one small broken piece, calling it the most satisfactory shipment from the condition standpoint. Rudolf replies that some models were complicated to unpack and explicitly credits Louis Bierweiler's skill and carefulness.

The result is a packing–unpacking loop: workshop support and immobilisation → customs opening → skilled unpacking/remounting → condition report → revised future packing.

## Customs and regulatory archives

The project now has targeted government archival universes rather than generic references to `customs`:

- Sächsisches Staatsarchiv: `11177 Oberfinanzpräsident Dresden`, together with post-1919 customs-office holdings, for the 1923 German export-permission, valuation and physical customs-inspection problem.
- U.S. National Archives, Record Group 36, Records of the U.S. Customs Service, especially Boston, for customs entry, bonded movement, inspection and port records around 1906, 1923 and 1929.
- U.S. National Archives, Record Group 7, Bureau of Entomology and Plant Quarantine and predecessors, for the 1929 problem in which straw/hay packing became regulated plant material.

The interpretive guard is central: the administrative category changed with the agency. The same consignment could be treated as scientific/artistic work for export valuation, glassware by a customs broker, bonded freight in transit and plant-risk material because of its packing.

## Payment and banking

`payment-regulation-register.json` adds a parallel infrastructure for money.

Early Ware correspondence records large bank drafts and receipt procedures. A 1904 draft OCR-read as drawn on `Dredner Bank` is not silently normalised to Dresdner Bank. Commerzbank's Historical Archive is retained only as a conditional target because it holds roughly 7,100 linear metres of Dresdner Bank records from 1872–2009; the primary image must first establish the bank identity.

The strongest sequence is 1934. Francis G. Goodale wrote that Rudolf instructed him to convert securities into cash and deliver the proceeds to the First National Bank of Boston for the account specified by the Reichsbank. A 13 September affidavit records $6,000 received, $4,950 paid out and a $1,050 balance transmitted through the Boston bank for credit to Rudolf's Reichsbank account under German law. Bundesarchiv guidance identifies `R 2501 — Deutsche Reichsbank` as the correct institutional fonds for testing whether the regulatory side survives outside the Blaschka archive. No complete First National Bank of Boston corporate archive was located in this public-catalogue pass.

## New source architecture

This pass changes the research unit. `Shipment` is no longer a single event between Dresden and a museum. The sources support a sequence of translations:

- models become temporarily mounted and packed units;
- cases become freight with marks, routes, weights and charges;
- freight becomes customs entries, bonded movement or quarantine material;
- arrival becomes skilled unpacking, minor repair and remounting;
- receiver experience becomes feedback for the next workshop shipment;
- payment becomes draft, bank receipt, currency conversion, securities and regulated foreign-exchange transfer.

This is now a source-rich infrastructure history rather than a generic circulation narrative.
