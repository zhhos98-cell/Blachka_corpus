Journey dossier · 1892

A workshop on the move

Rudolf Blaschka’s journey is mapped here as a knowledge-production system. The numbered route establishes chronology, including timed transport windows where the evidence permits; the other views show where observation, drawing, preservation, circulation, coordination, repair and workshop reassembly occurred.

Fieldwork did not simply precede the workshop. In 1892, workshop practice was distributed across bodies, gardens, drawings, specimens, railways, steamships, post and telegraph. The April 24–25 Arizona–California sequence also shows railway time becoming part of the observational infrastructure.

Three ways to read the same journey

Journey follows Rudolf’s documented movement. The dashed route preserves the itinerary that circulated publicly even when the traveller had already moved on.

Numbers are chronological sequence markers, not confidence rankings. Exact times come from dated correspondence where available; close but non-contemporaneous timetable values are labelled as contextual controls. The dashed public itinerary is retained only in Journey mode. Flow lines identify documented or explicitly prospective transfers and remote coordination rather than a second travel route. Gold S diamonds are separate visual-evidence markers: they show public-domain historical views that closely match landscapes recorded in Rudolf’s sketchbook without turning those views into numbered itinerary stops. Method

The route and the knowledge chain are kept separate.

The documented journey, the publicly circulated itinerary, the movement of plant material, and the movement of instructions are different evidential objects. They are shown together only when their difference remains visible. A dated place can prove presence without proving a separate collecting excursion; a timetable can constrain an observation window without proving an exact train unless the issue and date match. The sketchbook-match diamonds are likewise evidential controls rather than route nodes: a historical view can strongly support landscape identification while leaving the exact drawing point unresolved. Unresolved locations remain outside the secure route. Dates and identifications are corrected in the public view only when the archival layer or a cited control source supports the correction; the preserved OCR remains unchanged in the research backend.

References & source routes

Follow the evidence; reproduce only rights-safe controls.

Rights-restricted archival images and published figures remain linked to their holding institutions or publication pages. The map reproduces only public-domain historical control images where they make a close sketchbook landscape match visible. Rudolf’s sketchbook itself remains linked rather than reproduced here. The Corning study is used as a secondary control layer; the correspondence remains the primary chronological backbone.

Secondary study

Rossi-Wilcox, Susan M. “The Botanical Models (1886–1936).” In Drawing upon Nature: Studies for the Blaschkas’ Glass Models, selected and introduced by Susan M. Rossi-Wilcox and David Whitehouse, 20–33. Corning, NY: The Corning Museum of Glass, 2007. ISBN 9780872901667.

Used for chronology checks, selected identifications, and the published pairing of Castleton Gardens, Rudolf’s 1892 Jacaranda filicifolia drawing, and the later glass model.

Primary visual source

Rakow catalogue metadata identifies the beginning sketch as “Tempe, Arizona, 22. April 1892.” The current 30-page PDF instead has Maricopa, 24 April as its earliest clearly legible dated landscape and contains a duplicated image frame, so the catalogue/digital-surrogate discrepancy remains open.

Same-year timetable control

Arizona Republican, 29 May 1892, Southern Pacific railway timetable.

Shows the westbound Southern Pacific service passing Maricopa at about 6:35 a.m. Because the table is five weeks later than Rudolf’s 24 April journey, the time is used as close contextual control rather than exact-day proof.
