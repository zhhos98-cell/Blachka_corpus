## Baseline already established

Susan M. Rossi-Wilcox's 2007 chapter, “The botanical models (1886–1936),” already reconstructs Rudolf's 1892 field season in considerable detail and explicitly rests on transcriptions/translations of the 1892 letters. It also notes that botanic gardens saved field time, that seeds/cuttings extended the value of the trip, and that material from the season fed subsequent glass-model production. Therefore **a more granular itinerary is not, by itself, a sufficient intervention**.

The route remains useful as a temporal spine. The stronger question is what the route was doing materially and epistemically.

## Analytical shift from route to conversion infrastructure

The primary letters support treating the expedition as a **distributed conversion infrastructure** or **reference ecology**. The working chain is:

`living plant → field observation → drawing/color study → alcohol specimen / herbarium specimen → seed / bulb / live cutting → cultivation or supplier follow-up → glass model → Harvard display → repair/maintenance feedback`

This shifts the center of gravity from “Rudolf saw American flora directly” to the problem of how ephemeral, seasonal plants were converted into durable or renewable forms that could be used later in Hosterwitz. Direct observation mattered, but it was only one operation among preservation, drawing, transport, cultivation, procurement, correspondence, finance, and repair.

A stronger article-level claim to test is:

> The 1892 expedition reconfigured the Blaschka workshop from a geographically bounded workshop drawing on locally available and published reference material into a transatlantic reference ecology. Botanical gardens, field assistants, patrons, seed dealers, railways, steamships, preserved specimens, living propagules, drawings, and museum repairs became parts of the same model-making system.

This remains a working claim until calibrated against the secondary literature.

## High-value primary anchors

1. **Pre-trip finance and shipment — BLA-D00016 / 015:16.** On 20 January 1892 the Blaschkas acknowledged 4400 Mark half-year salary plus 1000 Mark for Rudolf's passage. Five cases containing 60 models and 329 analytical details were to travel via Bremen and New York to Boston. Field travel was financially and logistically attached to the Harvard commission before departure.

2. **Harvard as feedback site — BLA-D00676 / 017:30.** On first visiting the museum Rudolf inspected the Ware Collection in sixteen table-like cabinets, assessed supports and condition, and already intended to repair minor damage before going home. The museum was not merely the destination of finished objects; it became a technical feedback node.

3. **Delegated preservation in Jamaica — BLA-D00682 / 017:36 and BLA-D00704 / 017:58.** Robert Cameron accompanied Rudolf specifically to conserve collected plants. At Hope Garden Rudolf had a study room suitable for microscope work; Cameron supplied plant material, preserved it in white rum and water, and dried herbarium specimens while Rudolf drew and studied species. The expedition divided representational labor.

4. **Multiple reference media after Jamaica — BLA-D00725 / 017:79 and BLA-D00743 / 017:97.** Rudolf reported 106 species completed before leaving Jamaica. Back in Cambridge he sorted and mailed seeds, retained a live cactus cutting, sent cultivation suggestions, bought Jamaican landscape photographs, and immediately resumed drawing/study at the arboretum. “From nature” did not mean a single documentary medium.

5. **Ganong, preservation, procurement and finance — BLA-D00803 / 017:157.** Ganong helped conserve specimens in alcohol and prepare herbarium material and gave Rudolf seeds. Rudolf personally carried Arizona cacti to San Diego and planned to buy bulbs from Orcutt for shipment to Dresden. In the same passage he distinguished the Wares' payment of the trip and hotels from smaller plant/supply purchases and personal extras. This is a compact statement of labor, materials, transport and microeconomics.

6. **San Diego as supplier node — BLA-D00814 / 017:168.** Rudolf credited Orcutt and other botanists with obtaining plant material, reported 36 species completed there, and sent cacti and flowering bulbs onward. A local botanical network multiplied the yield of a short stay.

7. **Phenology as constraint — BLA-D00796 / 017:150, BLA-D00873 / 017:227, BLA-D00902 / 017:257.** Rudolf repeatedly evaluated places by what was actually in flower. Arizona happened to be visited at a favorable moment before desert vegetation disappeared; Colorado was too early for many plants; spring timing made self-collection of seeds difficult because seed season came later. “Route” was therefore a changing optimization against plant life cycles.

8. **Botanic garden substitution — BLA-D00896 / 017:251.** At the Missouri Botanical Garden Rudolf received a workroom. Darlingtonia, for which a Shasta Mountains excursion had been contemplated, was already in bloom there; he made a color sketch and preserved a specimen in alcohol. An identified living collection could substitute for a much longer expedition.

9. **Building future supply — BLA-D00890 / 017:244, BLA-D00902 / 017:257, BLA-D00927 / 017:282.** Rudolf deliberately searched for people who collected and sold seeds. He planned to discuss future acquisition with Gillett in Southwick, and by mid-June had received a Crinum bulb and seeds from Orcutt and wanted an ongoing arrangement for plants whose flowering seasons he could not wait for in California. The field season was designed to produce future access.

10. **Return as repair and extension — BLA-D00917 / 017:272 and BLA-D00927 / 017:282.** After cancelling the southern extension, Rudolf used Cambridge time to repair Harvard models, study local gardens, receive living materials, and plan ongoing supply. He estimated that drawing, studying and collecting had yielded roughly three years of material and said he had recorded everything that was in flower, “even the smallest weed.” Treat the duration as his contemporary estimate, not as a measured production period.

## Historiographical guard

Rossi-Wilcox 2007 already uses the translated letters and already narrates many of these episodes, including the Missouri Botanical Garden and the later value of specimens, seeds and cuttings. The contribution must therefore be analytical and evidentially cross-linked. The key test is whether we can show the **same plant or plant group moving across states of evidence**: field encounter → drawing → preserved specimen → seed/bulb/cutting → cultivated material → model/section → display/repair.

That object-level crosswalk would turn a suggestive infrastructural reading into a demonstrable historical mechanism.

## Source-critical guards

- Do not present the 1892 itinerary as newly discovered.
- Do not merge Rudolf's “106 species” before leaving Jamaica with the Jamaican institutional report of 116 colored drawings until counting conventions and dates are reconciled.
- Do not use souvenirs, timetables, passenger lists or retained newspaper issues as proof of physical presence without independent evidence.
- OCR translations are research surrogates. Verify image/transcription/translation before using a wording as a quotation.
- Keep direct observation, drawings, alcohol specimens, herbarium sheets, seeds, bulbs, cuttings and later glass models as separate evidential states until a specific crosswalk proves identity.
- Distinguish contemporary plan from completed movement; many letters revise itineraries in response to phenology, fatigue and available collections.
